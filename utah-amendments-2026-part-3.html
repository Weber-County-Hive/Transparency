Fact or fiction?

Claims we keep seeing in the comments. Pick an answer, then see the record.

New: Does Amendment B do what you think? Take the 12-question quiz →

1Amendment B applies to every citizen initiative.

Fiction

Only statewide citizen initiatives that include a tax increase, as S.J.R. 2 defines it. Other initiatives, tax-cut-only initiatives and referendums still need a simple majority.

2If B passes, the Legislature will also need 60% to raise taxes.

Fiction

B changes nothing for lawmakers. Bills still pass with "the majority of all the members elected to each house" (Utah Const. Art. VI, Sec. 22): 38 of 75 and 15 of 29.

3The Legislature needs a majority of all members, not just those present, to pass a bill.

Fact

Art. VI, Sec. 22. In 2018 a cannabis bill failed 36–34 because it "needed 38 votes, a constitutional majority" (KSL).

4A statewide initiative can be put on a low-turnout, odd-year ballot.

Fiction

It "must be submitted to voters at a regular, general election" (Utah Code 20A-7-203, -208), held in November of even-numbered years (20A-1-102).

5The governor can veto a law passed by initiative.

Fiction

"The governor may not veto a law adopted by the people" (Utah Code 20A-7-212). The Legislature can amend it, though, by its normal majority.

6The Legislature changed all three initiatives voters passed in 2018.

Fact

Prop 2 by H.B. 3001 (Dec. 3, 2018), Prop 3 by S.B. 96 (Feb. 11, 2019), Prop 4 by S.B. 200 (2020).

7Prop 2, medical cannabis, raised taxes.

Fiction

Fees were to cover most costs, and medical cannabis was made exempt from sales tax (Governor's budget office fiscal statement, via FOX 13). It is still tax-exempt (Marijuana Policy Project).

8The Legislature kept Prop 3's full sales tax while cutting its coverage.

Fact

S.B. 96 kept the 0.15% tax (Ballotpedia) and cut coverage from 138% to 100% of the poverty level until January 2020.

9The Legislature's Medicaid rewrite was projected to cost the state more at first.

Fact

Fiscal analysts estimated a "two-year cost of $49 million," about five times Prop 3's projected $10.4 million shortfall (Salt Lake Tribune, Feb. 4, 2019).

10Voting yes on B will undo the 2018 initiatives.

Fiction

B applies only to votes "after November 1, 2026" (S.J.R. 2 text). It doesn't reach back.

11No state requires voters to pass anything by a supermajority.

Fiction

Arizona's Prop 132 (2022) requires 60% for initiatives that create a new tax (AZFamily). Florida has required 60% for constitutional amendments since 2006 (Axios).

12Salt Lake County alone can pass a statewide initiative.

Fiction

It has about 34.5% of Utah's people (USAFacts / U.S. Census, 2025). Initiatives also need signatures in 26 of 29 Senate districts.

13Utah once required initiative signatures from 20 of its 29 counties.

Fact

The Utah Supreme Court struck that rule down in Gallivan v. Walker (2002 UT 89) for diluting urban voters' power.

14Only Democrats voted against Amendment B in the Legislature.

Fiction

The no votes included 2 Republican senators and 4 Republican representatives, plus every Democrat (Ballotpedia, March 10, 2025).

15A majority of voters could vote to take your house.

Fiction

Utah's Constitution bars it (Art. I, Sec. 22). Government can take property through eminent domain, for public use with just compensation, with no public vote (Utah Code 78B-6-501).

16Utah's Supreme Court grew from 5 to 7 justices in 2026.

Fact

S.B. 134, signed January 2026 (Ballotpedia), over objections from the chief justice and the Utah State Bar (Salt Lake Tribune).

17Tax initiatives just let voters raise taxes on 'other people.'

Partly true

A statewide initiative applies to everyone who votes for it. Yes voters tax themselves too. Prop 3's sales tax is paid by every shopper, including the people who voted for it. (Prop 3 (2018); Ballotpedia)

18Amendment B lets the majority decide.

Fiction

Under B, a tax initiative needs 60%. That means 41% of voters can block the other 59%. Without B, a simple majority decides. (S.J.R. 2 (2025))

19Once voters pass an initiative, it's final.

Fiction

The Legislature can amend initiatives after they pass. It changed Prop 2 within weeks and Prop 3 within three months. Since 2024, changes to government-reform initiatives are limited by the courts. (H.B. 3001 (2018); S.B. 96 (2019); League of Women Voters v. Utah Legislature (2024))

20Citizens keep raising taxes by initiative.

Fiction

Utah voters have approved 7 citizen initiatives, ever. Only 1 raised a tax: Prop 3 (2018). The Legislature kept that tax. Since 2015 alone, the Legislature has passed at least 9 tax increases or new taxing powers. (Ballotpedia Historical Ballot Measures Factbook; FOX 13; le.utah.gov)

Comments and responses, checked

Private tips are logged without names on the Tips we checked page, where you can also send one.

Claims from public Facebook debates in October 2026. Commenters are identified by initials only, and only when a name was available. Where a response was posted, its record-based points are summarized.

Rep. Tiara Auxier (R, House District 4) · Facebook comment · Oct. 12, 2026 · sitting legislators are named
"Had it been kept in place unaltered, it would've bankrupted our state… Health and Human Services is about to overtake [education] primarily because of Medicaid Expansion… If the legislature wants to pass a constitutional amendment, it needs 60% in both chambers… Much of the tax structure is actually constitutional… I know there is a Rep who has filed a bill to put the legislature under the same restriction."
Partly right; several points off

"Bankrupted our state": a prediction, not a record. The 2017 state estimate printed with Prop 3 projected about $77 million a year in costs and $90 million from the 0.15% sales tax by fiscal 2021, and warned costs "could outpace new revenue" later (vote.utah.gov). The Legislature's consensus forecast found the expansion fund's ongoing revenue may exceed its costs by $123 million in FY 2024 and $117 million in FY 2025 (Legislative Fiscal Analyst, 2023). The fund holds $342 million, which a legislative economist said would cover all expansion costs even in the worst economic case (Utah News Dispatch). The program running today is not the unaltered Prop 3: S.B. 96 (2019) added hospital assessments as a backup and let the state close enrollment if money runs short.

There is a Medicaid shortfall, but not in expansion: a $100 million to $200 million General Fund shortfall is in the traditional ("Legacy") Medicaid program. Causes listed: forecasting tools that couldn't handle post-COVID changes, data errors from a new Medicaid system, cost drivers left out of projections, and rising healthcare costs (Medicaid presentation to the Health and Human Services Interim Committee, Aug. 19, 2026).

"About to overtake" education: depends on which money you count. In the FY 2027 budget, Social Services is 30% of all funds and Public Education 28%, so by that measure it already has. Counting only state tax dollars (General, Income Tax and Uniform School funds), Public Education is 48% and Social Services 14% (Legislature's FY 2027 budget briefing, Aug. 2026). "Primarily because of Medicaid Expansion" is not shown in these records; expansion is paid 90% by the federal government.

"60% in both chambers": a constitutional amendment needs "two-thirds of all the members elected to each of the two houses," then a majority of voters (Utah Const. Art. XXIII, Sec. 1).

"Much of the tax structure is constitutional": mostly right. Article XIII sets property tax rules directly, including uniform taxation at fair market value, which property is exempt, and the cap letting the Legislature exempt "up to 45%" of a home's value. Changing those takes a constitutional amendment.

The bill for the Legislature: no 2027 bill found as of Oct. 17, 2026. Rep. Auxier's own 2026 resolution, H.J.R. 20, would have required a public vote on tax increases at every level of government. It never got a committee hearing. See A 2026 bill that went the other way.

Surplus and shortfall at the same time: the state closed FY 2025 with a $0.27 billion surplus and ended the 2026 session with a $21 million structural surplus going into FY 2028. The same Legislature briefing lists a $150 million annual Medicaid shortfall for FY 2026–2028 as a known issue (Legislature's FY 2027 budget briefing, Aug. 2026). Both are true; the shortfall is in traditional Medicaid, not expansion.

Facebook post · Oct. 8, 2026
"I know why Democrats hate Amendment B… Republicans have a supermajority at the legislature… initiatives are another route. Democrats can use out-of-state money, unions, and advocacy groups to sell tax-increasing agendas to Utahns."
Partly; the record is broader

Votes on B: S.J.R. 2 passed the Senate 21–8 and the House 55–17. The no votes included every Democrat plus 2 Republican senators and 4 Republican representatives (Ballotpedia, March 10, 2025).

Who raises taxes: Democrats last controlled both the Legislature and the governor's office after the 1964 election (Utah History Encyclopedia); the last Democratic governor left office in 1985 (Utah State Capitol). The Legislature has passed at least 9 tax increases or new taxing powers since 2015, among them H.B. 362 (2015 fuel tax and indexing), S.B. 2001 (2019 food tax, repealed after a citizen referendum) and S.B. 169 (2024, 15% MIDA lodging tax). When it rewrote Prop 3 in 2019 it kept the full 0.15% sales tax (Ballotpedia).

The largest recent tax-increase initiative: Our Schools Now (2018) raised over $2 million for a school tax. Top donors were Utah business leaders, including Gail Miller ($250,000), Vivint ($150,000), and Kem Gardner's family partnership, Zions and Karen Huntsman ($100,000 each); its chairman was a Zions Bank executive (disclosures.utah.gov). It ended in a deal with the Legislature.

Outside money: out-of-state groups did fund the 2018 initiatives (KUER, Oct. 15, 2018). Outside money also reaches legislative races; see the outside-money card below.

Commenter · replying to another commenter
"In order for the legislature to pass a bill, an ABSOLUTE MAJORITY of the TOTAL MEMBERSHIP of BOTH HOUSES must vote for it… a citizens initiative is passed by a majority of THOSE VOTING… Despite that it still becomes law even though only a minority of Utahns voted for it."
Right; context added

Right: bills need "the majority of all the members elected to each house" (Utah Const. Art. VI, Sec. 22), 38 of 75 and 15 of 29, and a veto override needs two-thirds of each house (Art. VII, Sec. 8). In Feb. 2018 a cannabis bill (H.B. 197) first failed 36–34 because it "needed 38 votes, a constitutional majority" (KSL).

Context: before any vote, initiative sponsors need signatures from 8% of active voters statewide (140,748 for 2026) and in 26 of 29 Senate districts. A statewide initiative "must be submitted to voters at a regular, general election" (Utah Code 20A-7-203, -208), held in November of even-numbered years (20A-1-102), so it always shares the ballot with a midterm or presidential race. "The governor may not veto a law adopted by the people," but the law takes effect 60 days after the next legislative session, and the Legislature can amend it by its normal majority (Utah Code 20A-7-212).

Commenter · response
"Only 14% of eligible Utah voters participate in odd-year elections… if you get citizen-led legislation on the ballot in an off-year, you only need 7% of eligible voters to side with you… The legislature still requires an absolute majority. Therefore, the standard is higher for them."
Absolute-majority point right; off-year math doesn't apply

Right: the Legislature counts all members elected, absences included; an initiative counts those voting.

Doesn't apply: statewide initiatives go only on the even-year regular general election ballot (Utah Code 20A-7-203, -208; 20A-1-102), so there is no off-year scenario. Using the commenter's own turnout figures, a midterm initiative needs about 22% of eligible voters at a majority and about 26% at 60%; a presidential-year one about 30% and 36%. The turnout figures themselves were not checked.

Prop 2 correction: Prop 2 did not raise a tax. Its fiscal statement showed fees covering about $1.4 million of ongoing costs and a sales-tax exemption costing about $1.6 million (FOX 13, citing the Governor's budget office). Medical cannabis is still exempt from sales tax (Marijuana Policy Project). Prop 3 did raise one: 0.15% statewide.

Commenter
"A yes vote will help to stop the horrendous mistake made in 2018."
Not supported

B applies only to initiatives "subject to a vote that occurs after November 1, 2026" (S.J.R. 2 text, le.utah.gov). It does not reach back. The Legislature already changed all three 2018 initiatives (H.B. 3001, S.B. 96, S.B. 200). Going forward, only Prop 3, which included a tax, would have needed 60%; Prop 4 had no tax. See What voters passed vs. what changed.

Commenter · replying to another commenter
"Ballot initiatives end up getting serious push from outside Utah… 50% is just too low a bar."
Right for 2018; outside money also reaches candidates

2018 (KUER, Oct. 15, 2018): Prop 3 raised nearly $2.3 million, "the vast majority" from The Fairness Project (Washington, D.C.). Prop 4 got more than $500,000 from the Texas-based Action Now Initiative. Prop 2 got more than $150,000 from the D.C.-based Marijuana Policy Project; its opponents, Drug Safe Utah, raised about $766,000, mostly from a handful of wealthy Utahns.

Candidates (disclosures.utah.gov): Meta's PAC, Forge the Future Project, made $270,000 in independent expenditures in one day (June 11, 2026) for two legislative candidates. One New York donor gave $429,500 to Utah politics from 2022 to 2026, including $100,000 to Gov. Cox.

Commenter
"They've also restructured the judiciary last year against the objection of the chief justice and the UT bar association… They've already made ballot initiatives more difficult by requiring a certain number of signatures in 24/29 counties (which ended up sinking their own ballot measure to repeal Prop 4)…"
Mostly right; two details off

Judiciary: confirmed, but this year. S.B. 134 expanded the Supreme Court from 5 to 7 and was signed in January 2026. Chief Justice Matthew Durrant told lawmakers the court had "essentially no backlog," and the Utah State Bar said the bills would "fundamentally remake the state's judicial system" (Salt Lake Tribune, Jan. 31, 2026).

Signatures: the rule uses Senate districts, not counties: 8% in 26 of 29. A county rule (20 of 29) was struck down in Gallivan v. Walker (2002 UT 89).

Prop 4 repeal: right that the district rule sank it. The effort, led by Utah GOP chair Rob Axson, turned in over 200,000 signatures (160,000+ verified); withdrawals in Senate District 15 dropped it below 8% there (Ballotpedia, March 28, 2026). It was declared failed in April (Utah News Dispatch). It was a party initiative, not a Legislature measure.

Commenter · two replies
"If 50.24% of the population decided it needed your house, do they have the right to take it from you? … I don't believe the government should be able to increase also without 100% tax payer sign off."
Opinion; records added

Your house: voters can't take it; the Utah Constitution says "Private property shall not be taken or damaged for public use without just compensation" (Art. I, Sec. 22). Government can, through eminent domain, with no public vote, for uses listed in Utah Code 78B-6-501, including roads "including for access to a development," power lines, pipelines and power-plant sites.

Your taxes: B adds no limit on the Legislature (majority of members elected) or on local governments, which can raise property taxes through Truth in Taxation hearings with no public vote, as Weber County did for its 7.25% increase. It applies only to citizen initiatives.

Commenter · posted on two threads
"I don't know of any citizen voting that requires a SUPER MAJORITY. It wasn't even a thing in the US Congress until they voted, internally, to change their own rules…"
Not supported

Voter supermajorities exist: Arizona's Prop 132 (2022) requires "any initiative that would create a new tax to receive 60% of the vote" and passed (AZFamily). Florida has required 60% for constitutional amendments since 2006; in 2024 its marijuana and abortion amendments won majorities and failed (Axios).

Not only rules: the U.S. Constitution itself requires two-thirds to override a veto, ratify a treaty or convict on impeachment, and two-thirds of Congress plus three-fourths of the states to amend it. The Senate filibuster is a rule, as the commenter said.

Commenter · replying to R.B.
"All they'd have to do is say… 'We'd need to add a penny to everyone's annual tax to make this happen, so you need 60%…' The Utah Supreme Court, which they stacked with loyalists this year… Now they've picked 4 of 7 this year alone, and they even picked loyalists who've never even been judges before."
Court facts confirmed; the 60% trigger works differently

Trigger: 60% applies when "the initiated legislation includes" a tax increase (S.J.R. 2). The Legislature cannot add a tax to an initiative's text before the vote. The pressure point is S.B. 73 (2025): sponsors must describe how an initiative is funded, and the Lt. Governor can reject the description after consulting the Legislative Fiscal Analyst. The 60% is of votes, not signatures.

Court (confirmed): S.B. 134 expanded the court to 7 (Ballotpedia). Justice Hagen resigned in May 2026 after Gov. Cox, Speaker Schultz and President Adams announced an investigation in April; the Utah GOP was campaigning against her retention (Election Law Blog). Chief Justice Durrant retired Aug. 31. Jay Jorgensen and Stephen Dent were confirmed June 17 (25–4, 22–7); neither had been a judge (KUER). Matthew Bell (district judge since 2017) and Michael Menssen (no judicial experience) were nominated Aug. 28 (KUER). Whether any are "loyalists" is a judgment, not a record.

Not checked: "made up their own court," a second justice "up for retention," and the party-messaging claim.

J.T.'s reply to another commenter
"The wording clearly states 'any citizens initiative which includes new taxes or a raise in current taxes.' It would require a constitutional amendment for the legislature to change the wording."
Mostly right; exact text added

The text requires "at least 60% of those voting on the legislation if the initiated legislation includes: the imposition of a new tax; an expansion of an existing tax to include additional items or transactions subject to the tax; an increase in an existing tax rate; or for a property tax, a change to the tax rate that causes the tax rate to decrease less than it would under current law" (2026 amendment booklet, le.utah.gov). Changing it would take another amendment: two-thirds of each house, then a majority of voters (Art. XXIII, Sec. 1). "New tax" is not defined in the text.

Commenter
"Misleading. Amendment B is not for every initiative."
Right

B covers only statewide citizen initiatives that include a tax increase, as S.J.R. 2 defines it. Other initiatives, tax-cut-only initiatives and referendums still need a simple majority. The YES/NO graphic was corrected Oct. 7 (see the dated note).

Commenter
"What I want to see is county-by-county votes on initiatives… As it is Salt Lake County alone can overturn what the other 28 counties vote down."
Not alone; a county rule was struck down

Salt Lake County has about 1.22 million of Utah's 3.54 million people, about 34.5% (USAFacts / U.S. Census, 2025), so it cannot reach 50% or 60% by itself. Utah once required initiative signatures in 20 of 29 counties; the Utah Supreme Court struck that down in Gallivan v. Walker (2002 UT 89), finding it was "diluting the power of urban registered voters and heightening the power of rural registered voters." Today's rule uses Senate districts, which have roughly equal populations.

Commenters · three replies
"I don't trust the initiatives… initiatives need a higher threshold." / "This is a tough one." / "When a minority can VETO a bill that is NOT a democracy."
Opinions; scope and math added

Scope: B raises the bar only for initiatives that include a tax increase; other initiatives and referendums are unchanged. Initiatives already face signature, funding-description (S.B. 73) and post-passage amendment checks. Amendment A is about how amendments are published, not initiative thresholds; it passed 70–0 and 29–0.

Math: under B, 41% of voters could block a tax-increase initiative. Both sides on record: "Allowing 40% of the population to block some of these policies just doesn't seem right" (Utahns Protecting Our Constitution); "If you want to raise your neighbors' taxes, you should have broad support from your neighbors" (Sen. Fillmore and Rep. Kyle). Both in Axios, Oct. 7, 2026.

Commenter
"I would like to see any initiative, whether it's citizen led or representative lead, that drives a penny of increase to the public to require 100% in favor… We are taxed to death."
Opinion; records added

No route to that exists on this ballot. A constitutional rule would have to start in the Legislature with two-thirds of each house (Art. XXIII, Sec. 1); citizens can't put amendments on the ballot. A 100% rule would let a single "no" stop any increase. For balance: the Legislature cut the income tax each year from 2022 to 2026 and passed at least 9 tax increases or new taxing powers since 2015 (see cuts vs. increases).

Follow-up question in the thread
"How many tax increases has the Legislature passed?"
At least 9 since 2015 (not a full count)

No official running total exists. From bills verified on le.utah.gov for this page, 7 raised a tax rate or created a new tax: H.B. 362 (2015, fuel tax plus inflation indexing; House 44–29, Senate 20–8), S.B. 2001 (2019, food tax; repealed Jan. 2020 after a referendum), S.B. 169 (2024, MIDA 15% accommodations tax; Senate 20–4, House 65–2), S.B. 272 (2024, up to 0.5% Salt Lake City sales tax), S.B. 333 (2025, sporting-venue tax tool), S.B. 242 (2026, path to Weber County's 0.20% sales tax) and H.B. 575 (2026, alternative-fuel tax raised while the gas tax was set lower).

2 created new taxing powers: H.B. 562 (2024, UFAIR: energy up to 6%, telecom 3.5%, lodging up to 15%, up to 35 years) and S.B. 233 (2021, MIDA's PIDs may levy property tax).

Most other tools on this page (TIF zones, CRAs, PIDs) redirect existing tax money or let a local body decide, so they are not counted here. The same Legislature also cut the income tax each year from 2022 through 2026. See Tax tools they created and cuts vs. increases.

Commenter · question
"Who is passing all these voter passed tax initiatives??"
Rare, per the record

Ballotpedia counts 28 citizen initiatives on Utah's statewide ballot from 1895 to 2024; from 1952 to 2022, 7 of 23 passed. No citizen initiative has qualified since 2018.

The most recent tax increase voters passed by initiative is Prop 3 (2018), Medicaid expansion with a 0.15% sales tax, at about 53%. That is under 60%, so it would have failed under Amendment B. The Legislature changed it with S.B. 96 (signed Feb. 11, 2019).

Often mixed up with it: Our Schools Now (2018) stopped gathering signatures after a deal with the Legislature (H.B. 293 and H.J.R. 20), so it never reached the ballot. Question 1 (2018, 10¢ gas tax) was a non-binding question the Legislature placed on the ballot; voters rejected it 65.45% to 34.55%. Initiative A (1988) would have capped property taxes and was defeated with 61.3% voting no. (Ballotpedia)

This covers recent history; every initiative back to 1895 has not been checked.

Commenter · question
"If this amendment doesn't pass by 60%, you'll repeal it, right? I mean, 50% + 1 applies here too, right?"
Yes, 50% + 1 applies to B

Every constitutional amendment, B included, takes effect "if a majority of the electors voting thereon shall approve the same" (Utah Const. Art. XXIII, Sec. 1). The 60% rule applies only to statewide citizen initiatives that include a tax increase, not to amendments.

Repeal is different: citizens in Utah cannot put a constitutional amendment on the ballot. A repeal of B would first need "two-thirds of all the members elected to each of the two houses" of the Legislature, then a simple majority of voters (Art. XXIII, Sec. 1).

Commenter · response
"A tax increase is a tax increase. Just because it's on a business… doesn't mean it doesn't affect consumers… I would also vote yes on requiring the same for legislators, but that's not on the ballot."
Right on the record; the rest is opinion

Business taxes: right that the rule doesn't depend on who pays. Under S.J.R. 2, any initiative that includes a new tax, a tax on more items, a higher rate, or a property-tax rate that falls less than current law would needs 60%, business tax or not. Effects on consumers and jobs are an economic argument, not something the records here settle.

Legislators: right, it's not on the ballot. A bill passes with "the majority of all the members elected to each house" (Utah Const. Art. VI, Sec. 22): 38 of 75 in the House, 15 of 29 in the Senate, tax increases included. Examples: H.B. 362 (2015) 44–29 / 20–8; S.B. 2001 (2019) 43–27 / 20–7; H.B. 562 (2024) 51–21 / 25–4. For comparison only, 60% of the 75 House members would be 45 votes.

Commenter · question
"If the Legislature tried to increase the food tax, they could do so by a simple majority… unless it got vetoed, in which case they would need a 60% supermajority to override… and we could gather signatures to put an initiative to remove this tax on the ballot… we would only need 50%, right? What am I missing?"
Mostly right

Right: the Legislature raises taxes by a majority of each house, with or without B. An initiative that only removes a tax is not one of S.J.R. 2's four items, so it would still need a simple majority.

One correction: overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each house, not 60% (Utah Const. Art. VII, Sec. 8).

What's missing: the 2019 food tax increase (S.B. 2001) was stopped by a referendum, not an initiative, and B doesn't change referendums. The Legislature repealed the law itself in January 2020 after the referendum qualified. An initiative that removes a tax can also be changed later by the Legislature, since only government-reform initiatives got the 2024 court's protection. And if a tax-cut initiative replaced the lost money with another tax, it would need 60%. See Words that sound alike.

Commenter · on the YES/NO graphic
"YES on B should say 'tax increase' not 'tax change'; NO on B should say 'The Legislature can only amend initiatives that do not alter or reform government afterward.'"
Partly right; page corrected

Tax increase: right that "tax change" was too broad. Plain tax cuts still need a simple majority. The ballot question says "increase taxes," and S.J.R. 2 covers a new tax, a tax on more items, a higher rate, or a property-tax rate that falls less than current law would. The graphic now says "tax increase, as S.J.R. 2 defines it."

Amending initiatives: partly right. The Utah Supreme Court (July 11, 2024) held the Legislature "can" amend a government-reform initiative with changes that "facilitate or support the reform, or at least do not impair" it, and that changes that do impair it survive only if "narrowly tailored to advance a compelling government interest." So the Legislature can still amend reform initiatives in limited ways, and the ruling does not limit amending other initiatives, such as a tax initiative. The NO column now names that limit.

B.W. · post
"In 2018 we had three [initiatives], and voters passed all three… The Legislature subsequently changed all three of them."
Verified

Prop 2 was replaced by H.B. 3001 (Dec. 3, 2018), Prop 3 changed by S.B. 96 (Feb. 11, 2019), and Prop 4 replaced by S.B. 200 (2020). (NORML; Ballotpedia; KJZZ)

Verified

Prop 3 included a 0.15% sales tax and passed with 53% (Ballotpedia; Salt Lake Tribune, Nov. 7, 2018). Because it included a tax, it would have needed 60% under S.J.R. 2.

B.W. · post
"The Utah Supreme Court… said… when citizens use an initiative to reform their government, the Legislature does not have unlimited power to simply undo that reform."
Verified

Unanimous ruling, July 11, 2024. Justice Paige Petersen: "When Utahns exercise their right to reform the government through a citizen initiative, their exercise of these rights is protected from government infringement." (KJZZ)

"Amendment D… was ultimately blocked from taking effect because constitutional requirements had not been properly followed."
Verified

Voided for failure to publish newspaper notice, and the court also found the ballot language misleading (FOX 13, Oct. 24, 2024). That newspaper rule is what Amendment A would change.

Partly true

Not supported: "all recent initiatives." Prop 4 (2018, redistricting) had no tax. Non-tax initiatives keep the simple majority.

S.M. · response
"Really everything is tied to a tax, so if they put that word in at all, it changes it."
Verified (as to funding)

Every statewide initiative gets an official cost estimate (vote.utah.gov). If citizens include a tax to pay for their own initiative, it needs 60% (S.J.R. 2). If they leave the funding out, the Legislature decides whether to fund it. Prop 3 funded itself with a sales tax.

Partly true

They are separate votes. The link: B does not change MIDA, UIPA or PID decisions, which had no public vote, but a citizen initiative making data centers pay the standard 6% energy tax instead of 0.5% (MIDA–Box Elder interlocal, §6(a)) would need 60%.

Commenter · comment
"Any increase or any new tax should have a higher margin to be passed. This amendment protects YOUR wallet."
Partly true

B adds a higher margin only for citizen initiatives. Increases by the Legislature (H.B. 362, 2015 fuel tax; S.B. 2001, 2019 food tax), counties (Weber County 7.25%, 2024), districts and PID boards keep their current rules.

Commenter · comment

Not supported

A NO vote keeps the current rule; it does not raise any tax. In 2019–20 it was citizens' referendum signatures (152,000) that led to repeal of a legislative food-tax increase (Ballotpedia).

Partly true

Initiatives don't go through committees, but they do go through a legal review, an official cost estimate, 7 public hearings and ~140,000 verified signatures, and the Legislature can amend them afterward, within the limits the Utah Supreme Court set in 2024 for government-reform initiatives (vote.utah.gov; S.B. 96; League of Women Voters v. Utah Legislature).

"What about lowering taxes?"
Verified (as explained)

A plain tax cut still needs a simple majority. A cut paired with a new tax (swap), a loophole closing, a "same rate for everyone" measure, or a property-tax rate held steady needs 60%, because the initiative "includes" one of S.J.R. 2's items A–D. Try it in Test an initiative.

Commenter · post
"The Legislature is not imposing the same 60% requirement on itself… Under that system, 59% of Utah voters could vote YES and 41% could vote NO — and the 41% wins."
Verified

The Legislature passes tax laws by a majority of each house, and S.J.R. 2 does not change that. Under a 60% rule, a 59% yes vote fails. Utah Const. Art. VI, Sec. 1 places legislative power in the Legislature and in the people through initiatives and referenda.

Commenter · post

Not settled for Prop 2: KSL and Axios report medical cannabis would also have failed under 60%. Prop 2's own fiscal estimate shows it created fees (about $1.4M a year) and a sales-tax exemption, not a new tax (vote.utah.gov, Medical Cannabis Act files). Whether fees count as a "tax" under S.J.R. 2 is not stated in its text.

The July 11, 2024 Utah Supreme Court ruling protected reform initiatives from unlimited repeal (KJZZ). The exact phrase "coequal in power" and the case it comes from were not confirmed for this page.

A 2026 bill that went the other way

H.J.R. 20 (2026), "Utah Taxpayer Oversight of Government Spending." Primary sponsor: Rep. Tiara Auxier. Its companion bill, H.B. 449, listed nine Republican sponsors: Reps. Auxier, Tyler Clancy, Leah Hansen, Ken Ivory, Mike Kohler, Jason Kyle, Trevor Lee, Karianne Lisonbee and Michael Petersen (LegiScan).

Amendment B and H.J.R. 20 both deal with who approves tax increases, and they point in opposite directions. Amendment B raises the bar for voters and leaves the Legislature at a simple majority. H.J.R. 20 would have made voters the check on tax increases, by simple majority, for the state and every city, county and district.

Why did it fail?

What the record shows

H.J.R. 20 and H.B. 449 were held in the House Rules Committee, which decides which bills go to a standing committee. Neither was assigned a hearing. When a bill is held there, there is no recorded vote and no stated reason (LegiScan bill histories, from le.utah.gov). The Utah League of Cities and Towns listed both bills in its Feb. 2, 2026 legislative policy packet with its position "pending" (ULCT).

What the record doesn't show

Why leadership didn't move it. A constitutional amendment needs two-thirds of each house, and this one would have limited the Legislature's own tax votes as well as local governments'. Whether that is why it stalled is not in any public record found. Rep. Auxier and the House Rules Committee are the people who could answer.

Which came first

Amendment B was not brought back after H.J.R. 20 failed. It passed the Legislature in March 2025, a year before H.J.R. 20 was filed. The same sponsors had tried the 60% idea twice before: H.J.R. 17 (2023) and H.J.R. 14 (2024). The other direction has been tried too. H.J.R. 1 (2011) would have required two-thirds of the Legislature and of every local governing body for any tax increase; it failed in committee. S.J.R. 9 (2020) was introduced as a two-thirds requirement for tax increase bills, but the sponsor replaced it before its first hearing with a different measure, "Use of Tax Revenue," on how income tax money can be spent. That version passed. The two-thirds rule did not.
