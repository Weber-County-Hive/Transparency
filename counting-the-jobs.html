Counting the Jobs

What Utah's data center deals promise, what the agreements require, and who the official numbers leave out

Part 1 checks the two largest Great Salt Lake data center proposals against their own documents. Part 2 asks who measures whether people can get work at all.

The Weber County Hive · We report public records. You decide.
Explain this simply

Picture a town that gives a big company a tax deal because it promises lots of jobs. Now picture the written contract. If the contract never says how many jobs, and never says what happens if they don't show up, then the jobs are a hope, not a promise anyone can enforce.

That's what this case file checks. It also asks a second question: when people can't get hired, do the official numbers even notice? Often they don't. Someone who gives up looking, or who starts their own business because nobody called back, doesn't show up as unemployed.

It also looks ahead. The computer chips inside a data center get replaced every few years, but the deals around the buildings last 25 to 50 years. If the technology changes, what's left is a building, and the question of who pays for it.

And it follows the money. Some governments collect more from these projects, some collect less, and some depend on the campus actually running. The "Taxes by place" section shows each one.

Draft vs. signed

MIDA has signed only two Stratos documents. The development agreement, the county agreement and the standards are posted as "draft and unsigned." This page labels every term by which kind it comes from.

Permanent job

A job that lasts after construction ends: technicians, security, managers. Construction jobs end when the buildings are done.

Clawback

A contract term that lets the public take money back if promised jobs or investment don't happen.

Tax increment

Property tax growth in an area. It is collected as usual, then a share is sent to a project or agency instead of the normal taxing entities.

PID

Public Infrastructure District. It borrows money for roads and pipes and repays it from property inside the district, not from general taxpayers.

LOC payment

Under the Stratos draft, computing buildings get a "Letter of Completion" instead of a normal occupancy certificate and pay 1.2% of their value instead of regular property tax. Most of it is paid back out (see the $100 chart).

Megawatt / gigawatt

Units of electric power. A gigawatt is 1,000 megawatts, roughly enough for 750,000 homes. Data centers are sized by how much power they use.

Grandfathered

Allowed to keep going under the old rules after a new law takes effect.

Decommissioning

Shutting a facility down and cleaning up the site: removing equipment, hazardous materials and sometimes the buildings.

Underemployed

Working, but in a job that doesn't use your training, like a college graduate in a job that doesn't require a degree. Counted as employed.

"But-for" analysis

A required explanation of why a project could not happen "but for" the public money. S.B. 206 requires one before new tax increment deals.

Labor force

People who are working or actively looking. Someone who stops looking is "not in the labor force" and isn't counted as unemployed.

Introduction

In the spring of 2026, Utahns were told a data center campus in Hansel Valley would bring 2,000 permanent jobs. By summer, the technology those buildings are designed to run had become the reason U.S. employers most often gave for cutting jobs. Both can be true at once.

Data centers are sold to Utah communities on two promises: tax revenue and jobs. When a community gives up land, water and tax dollars in exchange, the public can fairly ask what it gets in writing.

Part 1 checks the two largest Great Salt Lake proposals, the Stratos Project in Box Elder County and the Tooele Valley project in Tooele County, against their own documents: what was promoted, what the agreements require, who collects or gives up which taxes, what happens if the technology moves on, and which laws changed the rules. Part 2 asks who measures whether people can get work at all, how fast technology is replacing jobs compared with creating them, and who the official numbers leave out.

The 30-second version
  1. Stratos was promoted on 2,000 permanent jobs.
  2. The draft agreements require a 3-gigawatt power plant in five years. In the sections reviewed, they set no number of jobs.
  3. The yearly job count must be reported, but it can be marked confidential.
  4. Nothing reviewed says what happens to the buildings if the campus closes or the technology moves on.
  5. Laws since 2020 have pushed both ways: more disclosure in some places, new limits and tax breaks in others.
What "draft" means on this page

MIDA's board approved the Stratos agreements in April 2026, but they have not been signed. MIDA says it is waiting because of the referendum. Until they are signed, the terms could still change, or the agreements might never take effect.

Everything marked Draft is quoted from the versions MIDA posted publicly. Only two documents are Signed: the resolution approving the project area and the 0.5% energy tax.

What was promoted

The Stratos Project in Hansel Valley, Box Elder County, is Utah's largest data center proposal. Its developer, O'Leary Digital, has described it in jobs terms from the start.

In a May 2026 post, Kevin O'Leary said it would bring "10,000 jobs in the first gigawatt in construction, then 2,000 jobs to maintain it," according to ABC4. The Salt Lake Tribune reported 4,000 construction jobs on May 8; Forbes reported 10,000 on June 9. In a later KSL interview he described 4,000 to build and 2,000 to work there, said interested tenants are under nondisclosure agreements, and said the project is now a 10,000-acre campus.

What the benchmarks show

Forecasts get revised. Georgia's state audit first projected 28,350 data center construction jobs and 5,471 operations jobs. A January 2026 revision cut them to 8,505 and 1,641, about 30% of the original.

Big campuses need fewer people per unit of power. Industry figures cited by Quartz put staffing at the largest, most automated campuses at about 20 to 40 workers per 100 megawatts. For the "first gigawatt," that's about 200 to 400. Stratos plans its own power plants, and those benchmarks may not count power plant crews, security or support staff.

The point is not that 2,000 is false. No public document the Hive has reviewed shows how the figure was calculated, which phase it covers, or what happens if it isn't reached.

Within five years the developer must submit the Phase 1 plan and complete an energy facility in Hansel Valley "capable of producing at least three (3) Gigawatts." No hiring number appears in the sections reviewed.

A lawsuit to enforce is the "sole and exclusive remedy." Neither party may terminate or "pursue, claim or collect money damages." No clawback is described in the language reviewed.

State law

Utah's sales tax exemption for large data centers, rewritten by S.B. 114 in 2020, has no jobs, wage or investment requirement. The jobs-based credit, EDTIF, pays only after jobs exist at set wages; GOED reports one data center with it, Creekstone near Delta, projected at 106 permanent jobs. S.B. 114 case file →

That gap is where the risk sits. If AI demand slows, or newer chips need different power and cooling than a building was designed for, a campus could shrink or sit partly empty while the agreements around it keep running.

What the records say would happen
  • The draft Stratos county agreement has no provision for a campus that stops operating, sits empty or shrinks, and no cleanup or removal requirement. Who Holds the Risk? →
  • Utah's data center sales tax exemption covers replacement parts, so each new round of equipment is also exempt. S.B. 114 →
  • The county's $10,000-per-megawatt minimum is met by adjusting the 0.5% energy tax on power sold. If little power is sold, raising that rate raises little money.
What it would mean for jobs

Large campuses already need few permanent workers per unit of power, and newer ones are designed to need fewer. If a campus scales back, a job count the public can't yet see would shrink with it. Tyson's Eagle Mountain plant is closing about five years into a ten-year state incentive, ending 723 jobs; there, the jobs-based credits simply stopped. The Stratos drafts have no jobs condition to stop. Tyson case file →

The other side

An empty data center isn't automatically lost. The land, power connections and fiber have value to a new tenant, and older facilities have been reused. Retired coal plants have been converted into connections for solar and wind farms and into battery storage, and a Columbia Law School climate blog suggests states could do the same with data center sites. Whether that happens here depends on rules no one has written yet.

S.B. 114 (2020) · Sales tax exemption

Sen. Kirk Cullimore (primary sponsor) · Rep. Mike Schultz (House sponsor)

No sales tax on data center equipment or replacement parts; no jobs, wage or investment requirement. Cost still unknown to the state. Case file →

H.B. 77 (2026) · Reassessment thresholds

Rep. Steve Eliason · Sen. Daniel McCay · signed March 23, 2026

Flags homes for review at a 150% yearly value jump, non-residential property including data centers at 350%. A large facility would rarely hit that trigger. No separate fiscal estimate. Case file →

H.B. 507 (2026) · Limits on local data center incentives

Rep. Calvin Roberts · Sen. Kirk Cullimore · effective May 6, 2026

From May 6, 2027, no local incentives for data centers of 150,000 sq ft and 100 MW or more except through a new zone; existing deals can't be extended or increased.

But deals signed in the year before are grandfathered; new zones can capture up to 60% of property tax growth and up to 100% of personal property tax, with up to 80% used as incentives; an optional energy tax up to 6% can be returned up to 80% as incentives; a new protected records category covers business recruitment information shared among the state economic development office, the Inland Port Authority and MIDA; and the authority's revenue-sharing duty went from "shall" to "may." Case file →

S.B. 206 (2026) · Tax increment disclosure

Sen. Wayne A. Harper · Rep. R. Neil Walter · signed March 23, 2026

Since July 1, 2026, MIDA and the Inland Port Authority must hold a public meeting before a tax increment agreement or bond approval and file a disclosure with a public benefit analysis, a "but-for" analysis and a 40-year comparison of who gets the money. By Jan. 1, 2027, every existing project area must file its plan, budget, interlocal agreements and maps statewide. Open question: whether signing the Stratos drafts or forming its PIDs triggers the meeting. Case file →

S.B. 225 (2026) · Inland Port Authority water and incentives

Sen. Jerry W. Stevenson · Rep. Tyler Clancy · effective May 6, 2026

The 200,000-gallon-a-day limit on using property tax growth as an incentive was replaced, statewide, by each local water provider's own rules. The only recorded public supporter in the Senate committee was the Tooele Valley data center developer. The start date for increment payments is now set by the executive director's written order, not a board resolution. Case file →

S.B. 277 (2025) · Records appeals

Sen. Mike McKell

Replaced the seven-member citizen State Records Committee with a single-director Government Records Office. If yearly job counts are marked confidential, an appeal goes there.

H.B. 76 (2026) · Data center water reporting

The Stratos draft ties its yearly employee and energy reporting to this law.

Why there's no cost-per-job number yet

Cost per job = public support ÷ verified permanent jobs.

Neither number exists yet for either project. Stratos has no tenant announced and no jobs reported, and Tooele Valley has no job figure on record and is paused. The Hive will run it, with each kind of support kept separate, when real counts are public.

Part 2Who's counting the people looking for work?

What employers say about AI

Challenger, Gray & Christmas counted 116,175 U.S. job cuts from January to August 2026 where employers named AI as the reason, up from 54,836 in all of 2025. Those are announced reasons, not proven causes. Only 1% of laid-off workers in a Gallup survey said AI was the main reason, and Yale's Budget Lab found job churn still within historical ranges. The Bureau of Labor Statistics attributes no job loss to AI.

Replacing and creating: how fast?

Measured so far. Employers named AI in 54,836 announced U.S. job cuts in all of 2025, and in 116,175 in the first eight months of 2026, more than double in less than a year. Those are announcements, not proven causes. Stanford's 2026 AI Index found employment of software developers aged 22 to 25 down about 20% since 2024.

Who the new jobs are for

Growing fastest: farmworkers, delivery drivers, construction workers, nurses, secondary teachers, AI and renewable energy specialists. Declining fastest: cashiers, administrative assistants, graphic designers.

A net gain worldwide doesn't mean the same people move into the new jobs. An administrative assistant whose job disappears doesn't automatically become a nurse or an AI specialist. PwC's 2026 AI Jobs Barometer found entry-level roles exposed to AI are seven times more likely to ask for senior-level skills.

Data centers follow the same pattern on a small scale: thousands of construction jobs that end, then far fewer permanent, specialized ones. The forecasts are global and long-range. The Hive has not found a Utah agency count of how many jobs AI has replaced or created in the state.

The other side
  • Some rural officials prefer data centers because they bring tax revenue without large population growth.
  • Utah contractors and suppliers have won construction work.
  • Eagle Mountain's mayor says data centers fund the city's general budget. Meta case file →
  • Brookings found counties gain roughly 100–200 jobs from a first major data center; wages didn't change and home prices rose 2–5%.

Conclusion

The Stratos drafts do several things the public can check: they promise the county upfront and minimum payments, require yearly employee counts and set a hard power deadline. What they don't do, in the language reviewed, is require any number of jobs, allow recovery of money if the jobs don't appear, or say what happens to the buildings if the technology moves on. And the job count they do require can be kept out of public view.

Laws since 2020 push both ways. S.B. 206 now requires a public meeting and a 40-year comparison before new increment deals, and H.B. 507 limits new local data center incentives from May 2027. But S.B. 114 exempts every round of equipment from sales tax with no jobs condition, H.B. 507 grandfathers deals signed before its limit and adds a new protected records category, and S.B. 225 removed a statewide water limit on Inland Port incentives.

Nationally, the people most affected by a changing job market are the hardest to see in the numbers. Forecasts say technology will create more jobs than it removes worldwide, but different jobs, often needing different training.

Whether these campuses become steady employers and taxpayers, or large buildings that outlast the equipment inside them, isn't settled by any document reviewed. The drafts include no job number, no money damages and no cleanup requirement for the developer. The terms that would change that are ones the public can ask for by name: a job number, a way to recover money, public job counts, and a plan for the buildings when the technology moves on.

Questions to ask, and to whom

MIDA board and executive director

  • Will yearly employee counts be published, or marked confidential?
