Who Sits at the Utah Taxpayers Association's Table, and Who They Work For

The short version

  • The Utah Taxpayers Association is a 501(c)(4) nonprofit. Most of its money comes from members and donors whose names are not public: 72% of 2024 revenue, reported as contributions. From 2020 to 2023 its filings reported most revenue as membership dues instead.
  • Its 2025 scorecard listed S.J.R. 2, the bill that became Amendment B, as a "double-weighted, high-priority bill" it supported.
  • Board member Steve Young and longtime board member Mark Buchi are registered lobbyists for Holland & Hart and Intermountain Power Agency, both 2026 sponsors. Young also lobbies for Clyde Companies and EnergySolutions.
  • Four more board members share names with registered lobbyists for 2026 sponsors: Merit Medical, Rocky Mountain Power, Intermountain Healthcare and Comcast. No record ties those lobbyists to the board seats. Board member Cameron Cowan shares a name with the person the business registry lists as owner of the "Intermountain Power Agency" assumed name.
  • The 28 sponsors in our pull gave about $4.3 million to Utah candidates, PACs and parties from 2018 to 2026. Gov. Spencer Cox is the top recipient for most of them.
  • Most of its donors are not public. Two grants are: $15,000 from Americans for Tax Reform (2022) and $9,100 from IHC Health Services (2018).

What the Association is

  • A nonprofit since 1945. Utah's business registry lists it as a domestic nonprofit corporation, entity #658683-0140. Its federal filings show it has been tax-exempt since May 1945.
  • A 501(c)(4). That type of nonprofit can lobby and does not have to make its donors public.
  • Mostly donor-funded. Its 2024 Form 990 reports $656,435 in revenue. Contributions were $470,510 of that. Donor names are blacked out on public copies, as federal law allows.
  • Two funders are public. Grants made by other nonprofits show up on the givers' own tax filings. Candid's profile of the Association, which draws on those filings, lists two: $15,000 from Americans for Tax Reform, a Washington, D.C. 501(c)(4), in fiscal 2022 for "general support," and $9,100 from IHC Health Services (Intermountain Health), a Salt Lake City 501(c)(3), in fiscal 2018 to "support community health." Together, $24,100.
  • A 20-member board. Its 2024 Form 990 lists 20 board members as of Dec. 31, 2024, with Shawn Jensen as chair, Erin Barry vice chair, Jennifer Somers past chair, Keith Pitchford secretary, Nicole Lengel treasurer and Thom Carter legislative chair.
  • Its stated purpose includes developing "friendly relations" between people "lawfully engaged in business in the State of Utah" and state and local governments, school districts and other taxing units (its purpose and objectives page).
  • Its stated position on taxes. Its 2025 scorecard says: "Your Utah Taxpayers Association supports legislation which makes it more difficult to increase taxes."

How the Association describes itself to new members

Its member brochure, "Introduction to UTA" (a PowerPoint saved as PDF on May 26, 2021, with Rusty Cannon listed as president), says:

  • It is "the only statewide, nonpartisan organization" focused only on Utah's tax structure.
  • Its staff has "unparalleled access to state and local elected officials," and lawmakers "actively seek out" its staff.
  • It reviews the budgets of every county and school district and Utah's 50 largest cities each year. It takes a support, neutral or oppose position on every tax bill and gives a yearly "Friend of the Taxpayer" award based on lawmakers' votes.
  • Its endorsements come from the Heritage Foundation's Stephen Moore, from Jonathan Williams of the American Legislative Exchange Council (ALEC), and from Frank Pignanelli, who called it "the most powerful business association in Utah."

The brochure asks readers to "join," but it does not list members, membership levels or dues.

Its LinkedIn company page (viewed Oct. 21, 2026) says it operates "as a 501c4," lists Draper as its location and 2 to 10 employees, and says: "Members of the association include over 1,000 dues paying individuals, small businesses and many of the largest employers in the state." That page still describes members as dues-paying, while the 2024 tax return leaves the dues line blank. The page does not name any members.

Why member names are not public: the 2015 lawsuit

In November 2015 the Association, the Utah Taxpayers Legal Foundation and Libertas Institute sued Lt. Gov. Spencer Cox, Attorney General Sean Reyes and two county attorneys in federal court over H.B. 43 (2013). That law required nonprofits that spent $750 or more a year on political activity or ballot issues to report the names and addresses of people who gave them money, including membership dues, for publication online.

  • In the lawsuit backgrounder, the Association described itself as having "over 1,000 members." It said its work included "intervening in political campaigns – local and statewide," and that in 2014 it spent 16% of its budget urging people how to vote on two ballot issues. Howard Stephenson was its president.
  • On July 14, 2016, U.S. District Judge Dale A. Kimball approved a consent decree. The state agreed not to enforce the law against nonprofits doing what the decree calls constitutionally protected political and issues advocacy, and agreed to update its guidance. The Association's own release says the state "agreed that the law is unconstitutional when applied to these organizations."

That settlement is part of why no list of the Association's members or dues payers appears in state records.

For fairness
The groups argued, citing NAACP v. Alabama (1958), that the First Amendment protects the privacy of people who support advocacy groups. The case ended in a settlement, not a court ruling on the merits.
Two money lanes: one you can see, one you cannot.
  • The public lane: the Association's PAC. It registers with the state and reports who gives to it and who it pays. That is how we can show $25,714.57 from sponsors to the PAC and $57,125 from the PAC to candidates.
  • The private lane: the Association itself. As a 501(c)(4), its own money (72% of 2024 revenue, reported as contributions) is not reported to the state with donor names. That lane pays for its lobbying, scorecards and reports, and in 2014, by its own court filing, for 16% of its budget spent urging votes on two ballot issues.

The law, as settled in 2016, lets the Association's own spending stay unnamed, while its PAC must report. The same rule applies to every nonprofit doing protected advocacy, whatever its politics.

Who the Association named "Friends of the Taxpayer" in 2026

On March 19, 2026, the Association released its 2026 Legislative Scorecard. It tracked 53 bills and scored lawmakers on 10 votes. Anyone who voted with the Association at least 85% of the time, meaning on all but one scored bill, was named a "Friend of the Taxpayer." 34 lawmakers made the list: 30 in the House (40% of the House) and 4 in the Senate (14% of the Senate).

  • House: Ken Ivory, Mark Strong, Jake Sawyer, Logan Monson, Tiara Auxier, Trevor Lee, Rex Shipp, Walt Brooks, Karianne Lisonbee, Stephanie Gricius, Jill Koford, Ryan Wilcox, Mike Kohler, Matt MacPherson, Jason Kyle, Christine Watkins, Mike Petersen, Ariel Defay, Norman Thurston, Lisa Shepherd, Katy Hall, Calvin Roberts, Raymond Ward, Nelson Abbott, Jon Hawkins, Cory Maloy, Kristen Chevrier, Karen Peterson, Carl Albrecht and Candice Pierucci.
  • Senate: Lincoln Fillmore, John D. Johnson, Dan McCay and Brady Brammer.

All 34 are Republicans. The scorecard does not print party; we checked each name against the Legislature's current membership, and Trevor Lee against his 2026 session seat. Five of the ten lawmakers who received money from the Association's PAC are on the list: Fillmore, McCay, Wilcox, Kyle and Hawkins.

The Senate committee that hears tax bills

The minutes of the Feb. 11, 2026 meeting list the seven members of the Senate Revenue and Taxation Committee: Sen. Dan McCay (chair), Brady Brammer, Luz Escamilla, Lincoln Fillmore, Wayne Harper, Kirk Cullimore and Chris Wilson.

  • Four of the seven received money from the Association's PAC: McCay, Fillmore, Harper and Cullimore.
  • Three of the seven were named 2026 Friends of the Taxpayer: McCay, Fillmore and Brammer.
  • At that meeting, Association president Billy Hesterman "spoke to" S.B. 247, Road Funding Amendments. The minutes do not say whether he spoke for or against it. The committee also passed H.B. 272, one of the 10 bills on the Association's 2026 scorecard.

The minutes list every member under each vote without marking who voted which way, so we do not report individual votes from them.

For fairness
The scorecard grades votes, not party. Republicans hold about 61 of the 75 House seats and 22 of the 29 Senate seats, so roughly half of House Republicans and most Senate Republicans did not make the list. The scorecard does not say why no Democrat reached 85%.
For fairness: the Legislature has a large Republican majority, so a PAC that gives to bill sponsors and committee leaders will mostly be giving to Republicans. The Association's sponsors also gave to Democratic caucus committees. Its positions have cut both ways too. It supported the 2019 tax package that raised the sales tax on food while cutting the income tax, and it opposed redevelopment-agency retail subsidies and government-backed bonds for UTOPIA.

The Amendment B link

  • Taxpayer Shield PAC lists Sen. Lincoln Fillmore, the primary sponsor of S.J.R. 2, as chairman and Rusty Cannon as vice chairman. Billy Hesterman is on its board, with Bankers PAC president Howard Headlee. As of its last report it had one $2,000 loan in and $1,812.66 out, including $1,050 to the Utah Republican Party. No Amendment B spending is reported.
  • The Association's PAC gave Fillmore $6,500, the most of any recipient.
  • Americans for Prosperity paid for a statewide mailer supporting Amendment B (FOX 13, Oct. 9, 2026). AFP Utah is a 2026 golf sponsor.
  • Axios (Oct. 7, 2026) named the Association and Taxpayer Shield PAC as backers of Amendment B.
What we did not find: no record of the Association or its PAC giving to Taxpayer Shield PAC or paying for Amendment B ads.

For what Amendment B does, see The 2026 Amendments and Amendment B: Myth vs. Fact.

What the records do not show

  • Who gives the Association most of its money. Only two grants are public ($24,100 in all); its 2024 contributions alone were $470,510.
  • Whether the same-name board members and lobbyists are the same people.
  • Whether the Casey Hill on the board is lobbyist Casey Hill.
  • Philip Morris International's giving. Almost nothing is reported under that name.

Having lobbyists on a nonprofit's board is legal. The registry and the disclosure reports exist so the public can see these relationships. This page shows them side by side.
