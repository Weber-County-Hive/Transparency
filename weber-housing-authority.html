A County Commissioner, His Wife, and the Housing Authority That Pays Their Company

Gage Froerer and his wife Gloria both sit as trustees of the Weber Housing Authority. When he was appointed in 2019, his colleagues said so on the record — and declared, without explanation, that it wasn't a conflict. Over the next six years, that same authority paid $19,623 to two companies bearing his family's name, and neither the board seat nor the payments ever appeared on his own sworn disclosure form.

  • Weber County Commissioner Gage Froerer and his wife, Gloria, both sit as trustees of the Weber Housing Authority.
  • County warrant reports show the Authority paid $19,623 to two companies bearing the Froerer name from 2023 through 2025.
  • When he was appointed in 2019, his fellow commissioners said on the record there was "no conflict of interest," without stating a standard.
  • The Housing Authority seat does not appear on his 2025 or 2026 conflict-of-interest form.
  • Utah's disclosure system is meant to make relationships like this visible. It does not ban them.

Gage Froerer is a trustee of the Weber Housing Authority. His wife, Gloria, is also a trustee — and separately directs the Authority's own affiliated nonprofit, Friends of the Weber Housing Authority. Weber County's own warrant reports, reviewed month by month from January 2023 through December 2025, show the Authority paying $19,623 to two companies with the Froerer name on them: RESCOM INC and Froerer Real Estate and Property Management.

Weber County itself processes all of the Housing Authority's administrative and financial business, under an interlocal agreement between the two. That means the same county government Froerer sits on as Commissioner handles the payments a housing authority — where he and his wife both serve as trustees — makes to his own family's companies.

The Housing Authority's own office sits at 2380 Washington Blvd., Suite #112, Ogden — the same building as Froerer's own Weber County Commissioner office. It's also the same building where Highlander NP Housing, a now-dissolved nonprofit Froerer once directed, was registered. On December 23, 2025, the county approved a contract "for payment towards construction on Weber Housing Authority Office in the Weber Center" — funding the Authority's office space in that same building. Froerer seconded the motion and voted aye. Three months earlier, on August 26, 2025, he'd seconded and voted aye on a separate Memorandum of Understanding among the Sheriff's Office, the Housing Authority, and Weber Human Services for a supportive housing program — a second documented vote directly touching the Authority he sits on as trustee, again with no mention of that trusteeship on his own disclosure form.

The moment it was raised, and dismissed

The fact that both Froerers would sit on the same board wasn't discovered later. It was said out loud, on the record, the day the appointment was made.

On September 17, 2019, the Weber County Commission expanded the Housing Authority's board from five members to seven. Froerer was excused from that meeting — he wasn't there. In discussing his appointment, Chair Scott Jenkins told the record:

"He and Commissioner Harvey asked Commissioner Froerer to serve on this Board because it is in his portfolio and he does a great job, and that there is no conflict of interest that his wife serves on it." Commissioner Harvey concurred.

That's the entire record of the matter. No stated standard, no cited statute, no formal review — just an assertion, made by the two colleagues who wanted him on the board, that a conflict already known to exist wasn't one. Froerer himself wasn't present to weigh in either way. Everything that follows in this piece — the payments, the missing disclosure, the shared office building, the later votes — traces back to an arrangement that was flagged and waved off in a single sentence, six years before any of it was reviewed by anyone else.

How these forms are checked

The only person who fills out a conflict-of-interest form is the official it describes. Here is what happens after it is turned in.

What happens after a form is filed

From Utah Code 20A-11 Part 16, Weber County's disclosure act acknowledgment, and the Oct. 12, 2026 records appeals hearing.

flowchart TD A["The official fills out
and signs the form"] --> B["The form is posted
for the public"] B --> C["Review: was it filed,
and are the required
items filled in?"] C --> D["No one checks whether
the answers are accurate"] D --> E["Only a written complaint
starts a hearing"] E --> F["Penalty if a violation
is found: $100 civil fine,
class B misdemeanor"] B --> G["At-risk box checked:
employer is hidden"] G --> H["No proof requested
(county, Oct. 12, 2026)"]
  • The official signs, no one else confirms. The form asks the official to state that it is "true and accurate to the best of" their knowledge (Utah Code 20A-11-1604). No other person or office is required to confirm what it says.
  • The review checks that forms were filed, not whether they are right. Within 60 days after the filing deadline, the filing officer reviews whether each required person filed and whether the form contains the required information (Utah Code 20A-11-1605). The law does not require anyone to check the answers against other records.
  • Accuracy is examined only after a complaint. A written complaint leads to notice and a chance to be heard before the filing officer decides whether there was a violation (Utah Code 20A-11-1605).
  • The at-risk box is not checked either. At the Oct. 12, 2026 records appeals hearing, the county said Commissioner Froerer's 2026 employer line was redacted because of "an acknowledgement from Commissioner Froerer checking that box." No other basis was described, and county commissioners are not on the state's list of at-risk positions (The Weber County Hive, Oct. 13, 2026).
  • The written penalties. Not filing or not correcting a form within seven days after notice is a class B misdemeanor with a $100 civil fine (Utah Code 20A-11-1605). Weber County's acknowledgment of the County Officers and Employees Disclosure Act says knowingly and intentionally violating it is a class A misdemeanor and requires removal from office. In the cases the Hive reviewed in Utah's Campaign-Finance Enforcement Gap, including an earlier complaint about Froerer's disclosures, the result was an updated form and no public penalty.
Why it matters here: the Housing Authority seat has been missing from his form for two years running. Under these rules, nothing in the filing process would flag that unless someone files a complaint.

Why this matters

None of this is necessarily illegal on its face — Utah's disclosure system exists precisely so relationships like this are visible and checkable, not to prohibit every family business connection to public bodies. What the record shows is a repeating pattern on Froerer's own paperwork: board seats that come with real financial relationships attached, missing from the one document specifically designed to surface them, year after year.

Every figure above is checkable against Weber County's own warrant reports and Froerer's own filed disclosure forms.
