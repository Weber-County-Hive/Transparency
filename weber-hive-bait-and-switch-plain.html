What is this?

A bill has a title. Most of the time, the title tells you what the bill does. This series looks at eight cases where the final law did something different from what the title or the first draft said.

Bills often change on the way to becoming law. Most changes are small fixes. These eight moved toward less public say, less local control or less for the public to see.

Each case gets two grades. One is for power: does the public gain or lose a check? The other is for transparency: can the public see more or less?

Part 1: four bills

The food tax. In 2019, S.B. 2001 raised the state sales tax on food from 1.75% to 4.85%.

Citizens started a referendum to let voters decide. They needed 115,869 signatures and turned in about 152,000. The Legislature then repealed its own law with H.B. 185.

The lake commissioner. H.B. 491 (2023) created a Great Salt Lake Commissioner. But it left water rights with the State Engineer, and it added a records exemption for some water-right applications.

The protest grounds. H.B. 60 (2026) narrowed the reasons the state can use to deny a water application. Harm to recreation or a stream's health no longer counts.

The board. A 2017 bill created a balanced air quality advisory board. A 2024 bill repealed it.

Part 2: four more

The tax test. H.B. 236 (2026) first made a city or county show a budget without a tax increase before asking for one. That step was removed in a later draft. The bill's summary used the same words both times.

Two bills, one night. H.B. 125 and H.B. 157 (2026) had the same House sponsor. Both carried the same new power for the Department of Natural Resources to pay for water lawsuits. H.B. 125 passed without the $5 million. H.B. 157 died 17 minutes later, still holding it.

The school tech rule. H.B. 273 (2026) first said classroom tools must be "designed specifically for instructional use." A late change made it "demonstrated to have significant educational value."

The meeting rule. H.B. 17 (2026) removed a narrow "retreat" exception for PID boards. In its place, boards may meet anywhere inside the city or county that created the PID.

What repeats

The big changes came in rewrites and amendments, not in the first draft. Most passed with few or no "no" votes. The one case graded good for the public is the food tax, where 152,000 signatures changed the outcome.

What this does not say

This page does not say any law was broken. It puts each bill's first version next to its final version so you can compare them yourself.
