Why we looked

A social media post in September 2026 made claims about Utah's courts. It said the governor picks judges, the courts are exempt from open-records law, and complaints about judges and lawyers are hidden. We checked each claim against state law and the courts' own reports. Some parts hold up. Some do not.

How judges get the job

The governor does appoint judges. But the governor must pick from a list of at least three people chosen by a nominating commission. The state Senate must approve. After that, voters decide in a yes-or-no retention vote. A judge stays only with more "yes" votes than "no" votes.

Complaints about judges

You can complain about a judge to the Judicial Conduct Commission. State law keeps the complaint private while it is reviewed. Most complaints are dismissed. Some end in a warning that is posted without the judge's name. Only a few end in a public sanction.

Complaints about lawyers

Complaints about lawyers go to the Office of Professional Conduct. They stay private until a formal case is filed in court. Most are closed before that.

Who watches the judges

The commission has 11 seats. The law saves 3 seats for people who are not lawyers. Today at least 6 of the 11 members are lawyers or judges.

The 2026 test

In April 2026, the Utah House released records of a dismissed complaint against Justice Diana Hagen. The Utah Supreme Court said the records were released when they should not have been. The House Speaker said the House followed the law. Justice Hagen resigned in May. A new law, H.B. 186, now labels the commission's records "protected." It also requires a yearly report to lawmakers before Nov. 1.

Open records and the Bar

Utah's open-records law does apply to the courts. The courts handle records appeals under their own rules. The federal records law never applied to state courts. The Utah State Bar is registered as a nonprofit corporation. It says it works as an arm of the courts, under the Utah Supreme Court.

What's new

57 judges are on Utah ballots for Nov. 3, 2026. The state's judge evaluation commission recommended keeping all but one. You can read each judge's evaluation at judges.utah.gov. We do not tell readers how to vote on any judge. The first H.B. 186 report was not online yet when we checked on Oct. 17.

What this page does not say

This page does not say any judge, lawyer or office broke a rule. It shows what the public records say, and what they leave out.
