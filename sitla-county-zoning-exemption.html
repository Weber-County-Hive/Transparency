The Land Counties Can't Zone

A little-known Utah statute puts school trust land outside county zoning entirely — and the Governor's own staff holds a seat on the board that decides how it gets used.

Quick glossary — click a term
SITLA
The School and Institutional Trust Lands Administration — manages about 3.3 million acres granted to Utah at statehood, with a constitutional mandate to maximize revenue for public schools and other beneficiaries.
The zoning exemption
Utah Code §17-27a-304/§10-9a-304: state-owned property, including SITLA land, is not subject to county or municipal zoning "unless otherwise provided by law" — and that exemption follows a private developer working the land under a SITLA permit or lease.
Contract zoning
An illegal practice where a government body agrees by private contract to treat a project a certain way — skipping a public hearing, for example — instead of through the normal public zoning process.

Utah counties zone the land inside their borders — except when they can't. A statute most residents have never heard of puts state-owned trust land, including the acreage SITLA manages on behalf of public schools, outside a county's reach entirely. The same statute follows a private developer who leases or is permitted to use that land. And the board that decides how SITLA's land gets used isn't independent of the Governor's office — one of its members sits there explicitly to represent the Governor.

The Statute

+

Utah Code §17-27a-304, "State and federal property," governs counties. A matching statute, §10-9a-304, governs cities and towns. Both read, in relevant part:

§17-27a-304, Utah Code

(1) As used in this section, "property owned by the state" includes property owned as school and institutional trust land…

(2) Unless otherwise provided by law, nothing contained in this chapter may be construed as giving a county jurisdiction over property owned by the state or the United States.

(3) Subsection (2) applies to property owned by the state that is occupied or used by a person under a permit or lease.

Subsection (3) is the part that matters most in practice: it isn't just SITLA that's outside county zoning — it's anyone developing SITLA land under a lease or permit from the agency. A private developer working trust land under such an agreement inherits the same exemption.

SITLA's seven-member Board of Trustees is appointed by the Governor and confirmed by the Senate. Six come from a list supplied by a nominating committee; the seventh the Governor can appoint entirely on his own, with no list required. The board then hires the agency's Executive Director — but only "with the consent of the governor."

One current trustee, Mike Mower, is also Gov. Cox's Senior Advisor for Community Outreach and Intergovernmental Affairs — a member of the Governor's own senior staff. Asked about the role in a University of Utah law school interview, Mower put it plainly:

"I represent the governor on the School and Institutional Trust Lands Administration (SITLA) board, and we deal with a lot of oil and gas law and property law."

Mike Mower, S.J. Quinney College of Law interview

SITLA's own February 2026 board meeting minutes show Mower actively voting and making motions — this isn't a ceremonial or lapsed appointment.

Tested In Court Once

+

In Wallingford v. Moab City (2020 UT App 12), a developer's project near Moab needed modifications a local ordinance classified as "major changes" — requiring a public hearing. The city instead entered a contract with the developer and SITLA agreeing to treat the changes as "minor" and skip the hearing. Citizens sued. The Utah Court of Appeals struck the agreement down as illegal "contract zoning," ruling the city couldn't bargain away its own public-process requirements by private contract.

What this does and doesn't establish: the ruling shows Utah courts will police procedural shortcuts that route around required public hearings, even when SITLA is a party. It does not decide whether SITLA land itself sits outside county zoning jurisdiction under §17-27a-304 — that's a different legal question the case didn't reach.

What SITLA Says About Itself

+

SITLA's own administrative rule governing development transactions, R850-140, states its purpose is to let the agency "proceed in a more traditional, business-like approach rather than a governmental-like approach" — not the language of an agency that considers itself bound by the same public process a county follows.

But SITLA's own field staff have described a different practice on the ground. Asked about a contested sand-mine lease near Kanab, a SITLA mineral resource specialist told a local paper: "SITLA works with local communities and directs the permittee to go through local planning and zoning."

Both can be true. SITLA may not be legally required to defer to a county's zoning — and may still choose to, as a matter of practice or relationship management, whenever it serves the agency's own interests to do so. That choice can be withdrawn.

This Exact Parcel, Twice Before

+

The clearest real-world test case so far sits next to Wasatch County's Wasatch Highlands preliminary-municipality project, covered in the companion piece above. Philo Development sought a "legislative development agreement" from Wasatch County for 740 acres of adjacent SITLA land — 144 lots where current zoning allows one unit per 160 acres. The County Council rejected it in August 2026, and developer Greg Whitehead publicly called the denial "retaliation" for the separate preliminary-municipality filing.

  • 2004
    SITLA applies to Wasatch County for a rezone of this parcel. Denied.
  • 2019
    SITLA applies again. Denied again.
  • Aug 2026
    Philo Development, developing the land for SITLA, seeks a legislative development agreement from the county instead of a rezone. Denied a third time.

    • Twice before, SITLA went through the county's normal process rather than asserting the statutory exemption — a real, 20-year pattern of treating this specific parcel as subject to county review, whatever the bare statute allows. Whether that changes now, with a developer involved and a different political climate, is the open question.

    Water availability, not zoning authority, was one of the two reasons councilors gave for the August rejection — a reminder that a county's practical leverage over a project doesn't rise or fall on the zoning-jurisdiction question alone. Wasatch County has also used this same "legislative development agreement" tool itself, twice, to get things it wanted from other developers: a 75-foot community lodge at SkyRidge, and the LDS Church's Heber Valley Temple.

    What This Doesn't Show

    +
    What isn't established here: that the Governor's office, Mike Mower, or SITLA's board has ever deliberately used this statute to override a county's wishes. What's confirmed is the structural pathway — the exemption exists in state law, the Governor has direct representation on the board that decides how trust land gets used, and courts have already struck down at least one attempt to use SITLA involvement to skip a required public process elsewhere in Utah. Whether that pathway has actually been pulled as a lever, here or anywhere else, is a separate question this file doesn't answer.
