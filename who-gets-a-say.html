The short version

  • From 2018 to 2026, Utah's biggest political fights moved from who voters chose to the rules on who gets a say: the congressional map and who can vote in a party's primary.
  • The map changed a result. Under the Legislature's 2021 map, Republicans won all four U.S. House seats in 2022 and 2024. Under the court's 2025 map, Democrat Ben McAdams won District 1 on Nov. 3, 2026 (unofficial until the Nov. 23 canvass).
  • The party-switching law changed registrations, not any proven result. HB197 (2021) and HB365 (2023) set a deadline for switching from one party to another before a primary. No study found shows any race came out differently.
  • Money followed the map fight. The Prop 4 repeal drive reported more than $4.3 million from one out-of-state nonprofit. The committee against Amendment B raised about $1.28 million, about 44% of its 2026 cash from out of state.
  • Taxpayers paid too. The Legislature's outside lawyers on the map case were paid just over $1.859 million from January 2023 to April 2026, as reported by Utah Political Watch. No running public total is posted.

Utah's primary rules today

Each party decides who may vote in its primary. In the November general election, any registered voter can vote for anyone.

PrimaryWho can voteSource
RepublicanRegistered Republicans onlyKUER, 3/26/26
Libertarian, Constitution, GreenTheir own members onlySalt Lake County Clerk
Democratic, Independent American, ForwardOpen; unaffiliated voters ask the clerk for that ballotSalt Lake County Clerk
  • A voter can vote in only one party's primary.
  • Switching from one party to another: the deadline is April 1 in even years without a presidential race; in presidential years, the day after the candidate filing deadline (early January). A later switch takes effect after the primary (H.B. 365, 2023).
  • Unaffiliated voters may affiliate with a party "at a regular primary election" (Utah Code 20A-2-107.5, from H.B. 365). The Salt Lake County Clerk and KUER give June 12 as the date to pick a party and get a mailed ballot. How the two fit together is in Questions to ask.
  • Active voters (Lt. Governor, Nov. 2, 2026): 1,784,255 total; Republican 911,345; unaffiliated 512,711 (about 28.7%); Democratic 255,244; other 104,955 (vote.utah.gov).

Why the party-switching rule changed in 2021

HB197 (2021, primary sponsor Rep. Jordan Teuscher, R-South Jordan) set a deadline before primaries for voters switching from one party to another. Its stated aim was to stop "party raiding."

  • Votes: House 41–30 (Feb. 12, 2021), Senate 22–3 (le.utah.gov; KSL, 3/4/21). Rep. Stephanie Pitcher (D) said her yes vote was a remote-voting mistake: "I would have voted no if I could correct my vote" (KUTV, 2/12/21).
  • Sponsor's reason: he said "the potential for abuse is high," pointed to the 2020 Republican primary for governor, which Spencer Cox won over Jon Huntsman Jr. by 6,319 votes, and said more than 20,000 Democrats had switched to vote in it.
  • Critics: the United Utah Party called it "a transparent attempt to limit the number of people who can participate in Republican primaries."
  • 2023 follow-up: H.B. 365 (Teuscher; Senate sponsor Curtis Bramble) set today's deadlines. A 2023 special-session bill, HB2001, set a deadline for the District 2 special election and included $50,000 for voter-software changes (KSL/Deseret, 7/5/23).
  • No lawsuit against HB197 or HB365 turned up in searches on Nov. 8, 2026.
  • Signature: HB197 became law without Gov. Cox's signature on March 24, 2021 (le.utah.gov).

Did it change things?

The map change produced a measurable result. The party-switching law produced registration shifts but no documented change in who won.

Documented

  • Seats: all four U.S. House seats Republican in 2022 and 2024 under the 2021 map (Axios, 11/6/24); McAdams (D) wins the new District 1 in 2026 under Map 1. Initial totals showed him with at least 71% to Republican Riley Owen's 25% (KSL; unofficial).
  • A primary decided a seat: the 2024 District 2 Republican primary came down to 176 votes after a recount, Maloy over Jenkins (Deseret News, 12/26/24).
  • Registrations moved before the first deadline (Jan.–Mar. 2022): Republicans up about 24,000 net, Democrats down about 6,600, unaffiliated down nearly 8,900 (Salt Lake Tribune, 4/7/22).
  • GOP primary turnout: 67.7% of active Republicans in 2020 (527,178) vs. 47.65% in 2024 (430,137) (KPCW/Utah News Dispatch, 7/25/24). No source ties the drop to HB197.

Not shown by any record found

  • That crossover voters changed any primary before 2021, or that blocking them changed one after.
  • Cox won the governor's primary both times: by 6,319 votes in 2020 (no deadline) and 54.40% to 45.60% over Phil Lyman in 2024, with the deadline (official 2024 primary canvass).
  • Princeton's Electoral Innovation Lab (memo dated Feb. 22, 2021, using 2020 registration records) found 2020 Republican growth came mainly from new and unaffiliated voters, not from Democrats switching. Registration records can't show voters' intentions or ballot choices. Two BYU political scientists expected crossover voters to have little effect (Daily Universe, 3/31/22).

An auditor's view and a legislator's view

These are the questions each role would ask, built on the facts above. They are questions, not findings.

Auditor: did it meet its goal, what did it cost, what records back it?
HB197's sponsor cited Democrats switching; outside research found mostly new and unaffiliated voters. Unaffiliated voters, the largest source of new Republicans, are not covered by the switching deadline. The law set up no tracking or reporting, so "is it working?" can't be answered from the record. Legal cost: just over $1.859 million to Consovoy McCarthy, including $132,375 for expert Sean Trende, as reported by Bryan Schott (Utah Political Watch, 7/9/26), who cites the invoices. The invoices themselves have not been reviewed here.
Legislator: what problem, who do I answer to, will it hold up?
Supporters weigh a party's right to pick its own nominees, one simple deadline for everyone, and maps drawn by elected lawmakers who can be voted out. Critics weigh that the Republican primary often decides the seat, that voters passed Prop 4, and that splitting Salt Lake County four ways diluted its voters. Risks: the 2021 map lost in state court twice, a federal bid to block the replacement map was denied, and the bills keep coming to taxpayers.

What other states do

The National Conference of State Legislatures lists Utah as one of nine "partially closed" states, where each party decides whether unaffiliated voters may vote in its primary.

Map commissions elsewhere. Arizona, California, Colorado and Michigan use commissions, but lawmakers still have a hand in some. In Arizona, House and Senate leaders appoint four of the five members from a 25-name list drawn up by the state's appellate court nominating commission (Citizens Clean Elections Commission). In Colorado, legislative leaders supply pools of candidates for some seats. In California, four legislative leaders may each strike two applicants from each 20-person group before the state auditor's random draw (wedrawthelines.ca.gov). In Michigan, four leaders may each strike up to five of 200 randomly drawn applicants (Voters Not Politicians). A FiveThirtyEight review of the 2021 maps found commission and court maps less tilted toward a party than legislature-drawn maps, but not clearly more competitive, and called them "no panacea" (FiveThirtyEight).

Where the issues meet the money

  • The maps draw the most money. The biggest amounts on record are in the fight over who draws the maps.
  • Both sides use out-of-state money. Nearly all of the repeal money came through one out-of-state nonprofit. About 44% of the No-on-B money through Sept. 25 came from out of state, and about 85% of the cash it raised from Sept. 26 to Oct. 22.
  • Amendment B and the maps are linked, but only partly. Prop 4 was a citizen initiative, so rules on initiatives matter to anyone who might try another map initiative. But Amendment B's 60% rule covered only initiatives that raise or create a tax, so a map initiative with no tax would still have needed a simple majority. Amendment B failed on Nov. 3.
  • Primary rules show no money trail yet. No disclosure records were found tying spending to HB197 or the closed Republican primary. This is an open question, not a finding.

How does this measure up for the public?

Judged on transparency, financial accountability and the public good. The records here do not show that none of these changes served the public. A fair test asks four questions of each rule change:

  1. What problem was shown, with evidence, before the change?
  2. What public benefit resulted afterward?
  3. Who lost access or influence?
  4. What did taxpayers pay, and can they easily inspect those costs?

The strongest public-access finding. The Feb. 23, 2026 federal order (Gardner v. Henderson, No. 2:26-cv-00084) recounts the state district court's findings that in 2021 the Legislature did not vote on the commission's maps, did not make its plans available for public review and comment, and did not give a written report on how its map better met Prop 4. The federal panel described these findings; it did not make them itself, and it wrote that "there is no dispute" the steps were not met. These are the review and explanation steps Prop 4 promised residents, so they are the clearest place to ask how the 2021 process served the public.

Bottom line. Utah's disclosure rules work well where money goes straight to a registered committee. They work less well when money passes through a nonprofit first, when a group spends without a Utah filing, and when public money is spent on legal fees with no running report. Those gaps show up on both sides of these fights.

Options to weigh

Approaches used or proposed elsewhere, listed for comparison, not as recommendations. Each has trade-offs.

  • Use Prop 4 as passed, or one of the commission's 2021 maps, which would have avoided the lawsuits and their cost.
  • Build a measure into HB197: track switchers and report after each primary.
  • Open party primaries to unaffiliated voters by law, as New Hampshire and New Mexico do. Supporters: fairer to the 28.7% who are unaffiliated. Critics: takes away a party's choice of its own nominees.
  • Change the primary system (top-two or top-four). Supporters: every voter has a say. Critics: November can end up with two candidates from one party.
  • Name the original donors. Arizona voters passed Prop 211 in 2022 to require it above a set amount. The Arizona Supreme Court largely upheld it in June 2026 and sent one free-speech claim back to the lower courts (KJZZ).
  • A running public report of legal costs whenever the state is a party to an election or map lawsuit.
  • A filing for every statewide mailer or ad about a ballot measure, whoever pays for it.
