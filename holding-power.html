The short version

  • Republicans have held the governor's office since 1985, the attorney general's office since 2001, and two-thirds of both legislative chambers since about 2001.
  • Over that time, the Legislature drew the maps (2001, 2011, 2021), wrote the rules for petitions (35 laws from 2016 to 2026 in the Hive's count), changed or replaced all three 2018 voter initiatives, and in 2025–2026 reshaped how courts hear challenges to its laws.
  • It also created or restructured at least nine appointed boards and commissions from 2023 to 2026, with more in our research list; voters elect none of the members. One seat on a new state committee is set aside by law for a private industry group, 47G.
  • Voters pushed back: two referendum drives led lawmakers to repeal laws before a vote (2020, 2025), and a 2026 repeal of Prop 4 fell short after people removed their signatures.
  • On Nov. 3, 2026, Republicans were on track to lose about 15 House seats, which would end their House supermajority (unofficial).

Who gets on the ballot: SB54 (2014)

  • To head off the Count My Vote initiative, the Legislature passed SB54: caucuses and conventions stayed, and candidates could also reach the primary by gathering signatures. House 49–20, Senate 21–7; Gov. Herbert signed it March 10, 2014 (KSL).
  • Sen. Todd Weiler (R): "This never was about the will of the people." Count My Vote's Taylor Morgan: "We're thrilled with the legislation."
  • The Utah Republican Party sued. It lost in federal court, at the 10th Circuit (2018), and the U.S. Supreme Court declined review on March 4, 2019. The fight left the party with "hundreds of thousands of dollars" of debt. Then-Lt. Gov. Spencer Cox: "I do not get to pick and choose which laws to defend" (KUER).

The courts, 2024–2026

  • July 11, 2024: the Utah Supreme Court ruled unanimously that government-reform initiatives are "constitutionally protected from unfettered legislative amendment." Senate President Stuart Adams and Speaker Mike Schultz called it "one of the worst outcomes we've ever seen from the Utah Supreme Court." Gov. Cox: "I respect the role of the court in our system of government" (Colorado Politics; senate.utah.gov).
  • March 3, 2025: leaders announced a deal with the judiciary; bills on legislative input into judicial retention, a 67% retention vote and adding justices would "not proceed through the legislative process at this time." Leaders: "not an attack on the Judiciary" (KPCW).
  • 2026: S.B. 134 expanded the Utah Supreme Court from five to seven justices, over the objections of the chief justice and the Utah State Bar. HB392 created a three-judge "constitutional court"; when the Legislature, governor or attorney general is sued, it can move the case there (House 55–16, Senate 22–7; signed Feb. 13, 2026). HB366 lets any litigant ask for a three-judge panel (Utah News Dispatch; Deseret News).
  • New justices: Justice Diana Hagen resigned in May 2026 after the governor, Speaker and Senate President announced an investigation; the Utah GOP had campaigned against her retention. Chief Justice Matthew Durrant retired Aug. 31. The Senate confirmed two new justices on June 17, 2026 (22–7 and 25–4), neither of whom had served as a judge, and two more were nominated Aug. 28 (KUER). See Part 6 for how the Hagen matter ended.
  • Both sides: Rep. Jordan Teuscher (R): "The constitution is clear that the Legislature has the ability to create other courts." The Utah State Bar said the legislation "subordinates the judiciary." A Deseret News/Hinckley poll found 58% supported adding justices and 21% opposed (KSL).

Private groups with public seats: 47G

47G (formerly the Utah Aerospace & Defense Association) is a private nonprofit industry group, registered with the state since 2020. Its CEO, Aaron Starks, holds the Spaceport committee seat state law sets aside for "the state's largest aerospace and defense industry group."
