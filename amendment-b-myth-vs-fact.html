Start here

Today, a statewide citizen initiative, a law proposed by voters, passes with a simple majority. Amendment B would change the Utah Constitution so that an initiative that includes certain tax changes needs at least 60% of the vote instead.

It does not change the vote the Legislature needs to pass a tax, and it does not change any other kind of initiative.

The very simple version: Think of Utah as a classroom. The teachers (lawmakers) make rules if more than half of them agree. The students (voters) can also suggest a rule and pass it if more than half of them vote yes. Amendment B says that if the students' rule has anything to do with taxes, more than half isn't enough anymore: it takes 6 out of every 10 students. The teachers' rule stays the same.

Short version
  • Today a statewide citizen initiative passes with a simple majority. Amendment B would require at least 60% if the initiative includes a new tax, an expanded tax, a higher tax rate, or a property-tax rate that falls less than current law would.
  • It does not change the vote the Legislature needs to pass a tax (a majority of each house), and it does not change any other kind of initiative.
  • If a simple majority of voters approve it on Nov. 3, 2026, it takes effect Nov. 23, 2026, for votes after Nov. 1, 2026.
  • "Includes" decides when the 60% applies, and "current law" is not defined in the text.
  • This part checks 24 public claims, from both sides, against the record. This page does not tell readers how to vote.

What the amendment actually says

The question on your ballot:

"Shall the Utah Constitution be amended to require that citizen-proposed laws that increase taxes compared to existing law be approved by at least 60% of voters, instead of a simple majority?"

The constitutional text it adds to Article VI, Section 1:

"…initiated legislation that is subject to a vote that occurs after November 1, 2026, shall be adopted upon approval of at least 60% of those voting on the legislation if the initiated legislation includes:
  1. the imposition of a new tax;
  2. an expansion of an existing tax to include additional items or transactions subject to the tax;
  3. an increase in an existing tax rate; or
  4. for a property tax, a change to the tax rate that causes the tax rate to decrease less than it would under current law."

Two words carry a lot of weight. "Includes" means the rule turns on whether any of the four items appear in the initiative, not on whether taxes go up overall. "Current law" is not defined, and the text does not say when it is measured.

If approved by a simple majority of voters, it takes effect Nov. 23, 2026.

Sources: S.J.R. 2 (2025) enrolled text, le.utah.gov; Lt. Governor's ballot certification.

If you vote YES. If you vote NO.

Black and white: what each vote does, from the text.

YES

  • The Utah Constitution is changed.
  • A statewide citizen initiative that includes a new tax, an expanded tax, a higher tax rate, or a property-tax rate that falls less than current law would, needs at least 60% to pass.
  • Applies to votes after Nov. 1, 2026. Takes effect Nov. 23, 2026.
  • Other initiatives still pass with a simple majority. Wildlife initiatives still need two-thirds.
  • The Legislature still passes taxes with a majority of each house.
  • Local property-tax increases, PIDs, MIDA and UIPA are not affected.
  • To change it later, the Legislature must propose a new amendment with two-thirds of each house, and voters must approve it.

NO

  • The Utah Constitution stays as it is.
  • Every statewide citizen initiative, including tax initiatives, still passes with a simple majority.
  • Wildlife initiatives still need two-thirds.
  • The Legislature still passes taxes with a majority of each house.
  • Local property-tax increases, PIDs, MIDA and UIPA are not affected.
  • A NO vote does not put any new limit on the Legislature.
  • The Legislature could propose a similar amendment again in a future session.

Sources: S.J.R. 2 (2025) enrolled text; Utah Const. Art. VI, Secs. 1 and 22; Art. XXIII; State Democracy Research Initiative, Univ. of Wisconsin Law.

Myth vs. fact

Claims made in public discussion of Amendment B, from both sides, checked against the record. Names are left out; the claims are paraphrased or quoted as posted.


Claim
"Amendment B only applies to initiatives that raise taxes."
Partly true

The 60% rule applies if an initiative includes any of four items. That covers tax increases, but it can also reach an initiative that lowers taxes overall if it includes a new tax, and a property-tax rate that goes down "less than it would under current law." The text never uses the word "net." (S.J.R. 2, items A and D)

Claim
"Amendment B puts a 60% requirement on all citizen initiatives."
Myth

It applies only to initiatives that include one of the four tax changes. Every other statewide initiative still passes with a simple majority. (S.J.R. 2 enrolled text)

Claim
"We already require 60% for initiatives that change hunting."
Myth

The bar for wildlife initiatives is higher than 60%. The Constitution says initiated legislation to "allow, limit, or prohibit the taking of wildlife" is adopted "upon approval of two-thirds of those voting." Amendment B leaves that in place. (Utah Const. Art. VI, Sec. 1, as printed in S.J.R. 2)

Claim
"There is no check on initiatives."
Myth

Before signatures: Lt. Governor legal review; a cost estimate by the Office of the Legislative Fiscal Analyst; 7 public hearings in 7 regions with at least an hour of public comment each. Then signatures from 8% of active voters in at least 26 of 29 Senate districts, verified by county clerks. (vote.utah.gov) Since 2025, sponsors must also explain funding and whether a new tax is needed. (S.B. 73)

After passage, the Legislature can amend or replace an initiative, as it did with Prop 2 (H.B. 3001, Dec. 2018) and Prop 3 (S.B. 96, Feb. 2019). Courts can also strike down an initiative like any statute. (KUTV; Ballotpedia)

Claim
"Bills go through many steps to fine-tune them. Initiatives don't."
Partly true

True that initiatives don't get committee hearings or floor amendments, and voters must take the whole package YES or NO. But they do get the pre-ballot checks above, and the Legislature can amend any section after passage. (vote.utah.gov; KUTV; Ballotpedia)

Claim
"It's not the role of the people to legislate. Initiatives go against our Constitution."
Myth

Utah's Constitution says: "The Legislative power of the State shall be vested in: (a) a Senate and House of Representatives… and (b) the people of the State of Utah." Initiatives are written into it. Amendment B itself keeps initiatives; it changes only the vote needed for tax measures. (Utah Const. Art. VI, Sec. 1; S.J.R. 2)

Claim
"Voting NO does not create more restriction on the Legislature."
Fact

Correct. Rejecting B leaves the Legislature's threshold where it is. Neither amendment on the 2026 ballot changes the Legislature's vote requirement. (Utah News Dispatch, Sept. 28, 2026; Utah Const. Art. VI, Sec. 22)

Claim
"The Legislature's standard for raising taxes isn't changing."
Fact

Correct. Bills pass with a majority of the members elected to each house, plus the governor or a veto override. B does not change that. (Utah Const. Art. VI, Sec. 22; S.J.R. 2)

Claim
"We can work on a supermajority for the Legislature later."
Not as simple as it sounds

The Legislature's vote requirement is in the Constitution. Changing it takes a constitutional amendment, and in Utah only the Legislature can put one on the ballot, with two-thirds of each house. Citizens cannot start one. (Utah Const. Art. XXIII; State Democracy Research Initiative)

Claim
"It also makes it harder to reverse tax increases."
Partly true

An initiative that simply repeals or cuts a tax is not on the list, so it still passes with a simple majority. The exceptions: a cut that also adds a new tax (a swap), or a property-tax cut smaller than current law would give. (S.J.R. 2, items A and D)

Claim
"Replacing one tax with a smaller one isn't a 'new tax.'"
Open question

The text says the 60% applies if an initiative "includes… the imposition of a new tax." It does not say "net increase." If the replacement is a different kind of tax, it reads as a new tax on its face. How a court would treat a swap has not been decided. (S.J.R. 2)

Claim
"When is 'current law' measured: at filing, at signatures, at qualification or on Election Day?"
Open question

The amendment does not define "current law" or say when it is measured. That matters if the Legislature changes a tax while an initiative is underway. (S.J.R. 2 enrolled text)

Claim
"Raising the bar to 60% makes sure it really is the will of the majority."
Not what the math shows

Under 60%, a measure with 59% support fails, so 41% of voters decide the outcome. Example: if 1 million people voted, a simple majority is 500,001; 60% is 600,000, about 100,000 more yes votes. (S.J.R. 2; the 1 million figure is an illustration, not a turnout estimate)

Claim
"Outside groups fund, write and market initiatives."
Fact, on both sides

Prop 2 (2018) was backed by the national Marijuana Policy Project. (KUTV, Dec. 2018) Paid signature firms also work for candidates: circulators for Gathering Inc. and others were charged in 2025 over 2024 candidate petitions. (Utah AG, March 2025) In-state institutions shape outcomes too: in 2018, Church leaders opposed Prop 2 and backed a compromise that the Legislature adopted. (Religion News Service; Salt Lake Tribune)

Donors to initiative campaigns are public at disclosures.utah.gov. Amendment B does not address where money comes from: a well-funded campaign can still reach 60%, and a grassroots one faces the same bar.

Claim
"Outside groups oppose B because they have tax initiatives planned."
Not documented

No specific planned statewide tax initiative was found. Every statewide initiative must be filed with the Lt. Governor's office, so a planned one would be public record. The main group opposing B, Utahns Protecting Our Constitution, reported about $593,000 raised as of September 2026; its donors are public. (Utah News Dispatch, Sept. 28, 2026; vote.utah.gov)

Claim
"I don't want the Wasatch Front voting themselves things that rural counties pay for."
Partly true

Most voters live on the Wasatch Front, and a 60% bar does give rural voters more weight against citizen tax initiatives. But an initiative already can't qualify without signatures from 8% of voters in 26 of 29 Senate districts, and hearings are required in regions including Bear River, Southeast and the Uintah Basin. (vote.utah.gov)

B does not change the Legislature, or state authorities acting in rural counties. MIDA's Stratos agreement in Box Elder County sets the energy tax at "at least 0.5%" for data center campuses and 6% for other users; UIPA's Tooele Valley plan sends 75% of new property-tax growth to UIPA for up to 40 years. Neither had a public vote. (MIDA–Box Elder interlocal agreement §6(a); UIPA Project Area Plan, Amendment #2)

Claim
"Most tax increases go to a vote of the people."
Myth

Property-tax increases by cities, counties and school districts require a notice and hearing, not a vote. Weber County imposed a 0.20% county sales tax by commission vote, with no ballot question. (CASE 2026-STAX-01) Utah PIDs carry about $5.267 billion in debt no voter approved. (State Auditor Tina Cannon) School districts can issue lease revenue bonds without a vote; Alpine issued $201 million after S.B. 188 (2025). General obligation bonds still require a vote.

Claim
"Tax cuts never happen."
Myth

The Legislature cut the state income-tax rate five times since 2018, from 4.95% to 4.45%. (Utah House, "Tax Cut Streak," house.utleg.gov) On a median household income of $104,000, the 2026 cut of 0.05 points is about $52 a year. (Census via FRED, 2024)

Added Oct. 7, 2026: in the same years, other taxes went up or new ones were allowed: a higher fuel-tax rate under automatic indexing, a higher alternative-fuel tax, new sales, energy and lodging taxes for special districts, Weber County's 0.20% sales tax, and dozens of local property-tax increases. See Cut here, raised there.

Claim
"The Legislature keeps cutting taxes, so our taxes are going down."
Partly

The state income-tax rate went down every year from 2022 to 2026, worth about $101 million a year in 2026 alone. (Utah House, "Tax Cut Streak") In 2026, 67 cities, districts and other local taxing entities sought $93.58 million in property-tax increases, eight of them in Weber County. (KSL, Aug. 4, 2026, citing the Utah Taxpayers Association) Weber County's new 0.20% sales tax adds about $13.1 million a year. (CASE 2026-STAX-01) Whether a household pays less overall depends on where it lives and what it buys. See Cut here, raised there.

Claim
"Citizens have voted for initiatives that carried taxes."
Fact

In 2018, Medicaid expansion (53.3%) and medical cannabis (52.7%) both passed. Neither reached 60%. (2018 canvass, vote.utah.gov; Utah News Dispatch)

Split, corrected Oct. 7, 2026: Medicaid expansion included a 0.15% sales tax, so B's 60% would have applied. For medical cannabis, KSL and Axios report it would also have failed, but its fiscal estimate shows fees (about $1.4M a year) and a sales-tax exemption rather than a new tax; whether fees count as a "tax" under S.J.R. 2 is not stated in its text. (vote.utah.gov, Medical Cannabis Act files; Axios, Oct. 7, 2026)

Claim
"Ruling by initiative is what got California into trouble."
Different system

California lets citizens amend its state constitution by initiative. Utah does not; it allows statutory initiatives only, and the Legislature can amend them afterward, with limits since 2024 on changes that impair government-reform initiatives. The last statewide citizen initiatives to reach Utah's ballot were in 2018. (State Democracy Research Initiative; Ballotpedia)

Claim
"Amendment B also needs 60% to pass."
Myth

B needed two-thirds of each house to reach the ballot, and needs only a simple majority of voters to pass. (Ballotpedia, March 2025; S.J.R. 2)

Claim
"If it doesn't work, it's easy to undo."
Myth

It becomes part of the Constitution. Undoing it takes a new amendment, which only the Legislature can propose, with two-thirds of each house, followed by a public vote. (Utah Const. Art. XXIII; State Democracy Research Initiative)

Claim
"With 3.5 million people, 1% is a big enough buffer to prove a majority."
Right idea, wrong base

Utah has about 3.55 million people, but ballot math counts votes cast. Utah has about 1.76 million active voters (140,748 signatures = 8%). At 1 million votes, a 1-point margin is 10,000 votes; B's 60% requires about 100,000 more yes votes than a simple majority. (Gardner Policy Institute via KUTV; vote.utah.gov)
