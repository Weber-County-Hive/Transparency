The Weber County Hive  ·  Land Authorities Series  ·  Case File 01

The land nobody zones.

Utah's school trust land agency answers to a governor-appointed board, owes its loyalty to school beneficiaries, and — by its own general counsel's own words — sits mostly outside county zoning power. Here's how that chain of authority actually works, and what it looked like when a Utah county said no anyway.

Agency: School & Institutional Trust Lands Administration (SITLA) Governing statute: Utah Code §17-79-306 Case study: 740 acres, Wasatch County
01New to this

Five terms, plainly, before anything else

Start here if you're new to trust land
SITLA
The state agency that manages roughly 3.4 million acres Utah received from the federal government at statehood. It's not a park agency — its job is to make money from that land for schools.
Trustee
Utah doesn't own trust land the way it owns a courthouse. The state constitution makes it a trustee — legally obligated to manage the land for the financial benefit of specific beneficiaries, mainly public schools.
Beneficiary
Who the money is actually for. Mostly K-12 public schools, plus universities, state hospitals, and a few other named institutions. Not "the public" in general.
Board of Trustees
Seven people who approve SITLA's major land deals. The governor appoints all seven, with state Senate confirmation.
The exemption
A Utah statute that says county zoning law doesn't automatically apply to land owned by the state — including SITLA trust land. This is the whole story below.

The tension in one sentence: SITLA appears to have the legal right to ignore county zoning on its own land — and its own lawyers have said so on the record — but in practice it usually asks counties for permission anyway, and sometimes takes no for an answer. Both things are true at once, and which one wins in any given county is not settled by statute.

02Who's actually in charge

The chain of title, in five links

SITLA looks like an ordinary state agency. It isn't quite. Its obligations run backward through five distinct layers of authority, each with a different job — and each worth knowing before asking "who decided this."

Link 1 — Federal origin

Congress and the Utah Enabling Act

At statehood, Congress granted Utah designated land in trust for specific purposes — mainly to fund public schools. That federal bargain is the root of everything below it.

Link 2 — State constitution

Utah becomes a trustee, not an owner

The Utah Constitution locks that federal bargain in. Trust revenue belongs to the beneficiaries — the state can't spend it as ordinary public money, and can't treat trust land as an ordinary public asset either.

Link 3 — Legislature

Title 53C sets the rules SITLA runs on

State lawmakers wrote the statute that structures SITLA's board, its powers, and its relationship to counties — including the zoning exemption below. Change the statute, and the whole relationship can move.

Link 4 — Governor and Senate

Who appoints the seven-member board

The governor appoints all seven trustees, six from a nominating list and one at full discretion, all confirmed by the state Senate. Gov. Cox's own senior advisor for community outreach and intergovernmental affairs, Mike Mower, holds a seat on the board himself.

Link 5 — Staff and director

Where individual parcel deals actually get negotiated

Below the board, SITLA's director and real-estate staff negotiate directly with developers, water providers, and counties. This is the level where a specific 740-acre parcel near Heber becomes a specific deal.

03The legal question underneath everything

The statute that may put SITLA land outside county zoning

One sentence of Utah code does most of the work in this entire story.

§17-79-306 — State and federal property formerly §17-27a-304, renumbered 11/6/2025
"Property owned by the state" includes property owned as school and institutional trust land.

Unless otherwise provided by law, nothing in the county land-use chapter gives a county jurisdiction over property owned by the state or the United States — and this extends to a private developer occupying that land under a permit or lease from the state.

Utah's counties have known about this for at least a decade, and SITLA's own top lawyer has confirmed it plainly, on the record — while also describing a very different day-to-day practice.

What the statute says

"When it comes to state lands, counties have no legal basis for asserting a zoning preference."

John Andrews, SITLA General Counsel — Salt Lake Tribune, 2016

What SITLA says it actually does

"SITLA has gone to as great a length as we can to give local government input on land use... If the county says this is not the place for it, we take no for an answer."

John Andrews, same interview

Both statements came from the same lawyer, in the same conversation. Andrews pointed to a real example of the second half: SITLA pulled a Parker Mountain conservation-easement sale after Wayne County objected to it. That's the pattern that makes this genuinely hard to characterize in one sentence — the legal exemption is real, but so is a long practice of not using it.

A county that wrote the exemption into its own code

Garfield County's planning commission minutes from a May 2024 meeting on the "Promise Rock" SITLA property near Cannonville show the county amending its own zoning ordinance to state that zoning designations on SITLA-owned property are discontinued, and that SITLA parcels are only zoned once they're sold into private ownership. A Utah county formally conceding the point, in an unrelated dispute — one of the clearest real-world confirmations of how the exemption functions in practice.

A Utah county that flat out declined to act on this basis

A 2016 Salt Lake Tribune story documented a gravel pit proposed on SITLA land near Torrey, in Wayne County. County commissioners were advised that state-owned land is exempt from local zoning — and formally agreed not to act on the rezone request as a result. The Utah Association of Counties intervened to challenge whether counties have zoning jurisdiction over SITLA land at all. As of this writing, no Utah Supreme Court ruling has squarely resolved the question — it remains genuinely unsettled a decade later.

04The parcel this is actually about

740 acres east of Heber, twenty-two years in the making

A single SITLA parcel outside Heber City shows both halves of the tension above — SITLA negotiating through the ordinary county process for most of two decades, followed by a state official publicly threatening to skip that process altogether.

2004
First rezone request denied

SITLA applies to Wasatch County for a rezone of the parcel. Denied — the first sign this land has long been treated as subject to county review in practice, whatever the statute allows.

2019
Second rezone request denied

SITLA applies again. Denied again — fifteen years and the same outcome, reinforcing that both sides had, up to this point, been playing by the county's ordinary rules.

Feb 2022
A developer approaches SITLA

Mountain Venture Studios proposes a development lease on the parcel. SITLA issues a public RFP; no competing offers come in.

Feb 2023
Option agreement finalized

SITLA finalizes a mutual option covering the 720–740 SITLA acres plus neighboring private land — roughly 4,000 acres of combined project area.

Dec 2024
SITLA publicly threatens the override

At a joint Heber City/Wasatch County council meeting, SITLA representative Gregg McArthur tells both councils SITLA will move forward on the parcel with or without local support.

"We'll exercise our exemption, and we'll get affordable housing on this property... We're a state entity, and we can put what we want on those lands now."

Gregg McArthur, SITLA — KSL, Dec. 2024

McArthur also ties the project directly to Gov. Cox's housing-supply goals, and puts a number on the incentive: raising density on the parcel could take SITLA's revenue from roughly $3.6 million to more than $15 million.

Aug 2026
Wasatch County rejects the deal anyway

The county council votes down the negotiated "legislative development agreement" covering the parcel — 144 lots proposed on land currently zoned for roughly one unit per 160 acres. The council cites density and water availability.

Aug 18, 2026
A funding threat, delivered to the wrong room

Days later, Gov. Cox's senior housing adviser Steve Waldrip tells the Heber City Council — not the Wasatch County Council that actually voted — that county funding is "at risk, meaning none," citing the SITLA rejection alongside the county's separate opposition to the state's preliminary-municipality law.

Worth stating plainly

No public record found yet shows SITLA or the developer formally invoking the §17-79-306 exemption on this specific parcel. McArthur's 2024 remarks are the clearest evidence SITLA is willing to — but as of the county's Aug. 2026 rejection, the exemption itself had not been exercised here. The county's twenty-two-year practice of treating this land as subject to review is real; so is the standing threat to stop doing that.

05A common mix-up, cleared up

The Heber Valley Temple case is not a SITLA case

Van Dusen v. Wasatch County — the Utah Supreme Court case over the LDS Church's Heber Valley Temple — gets raised alongside this story often enough that it's worth separating clearly. The properties are different, the legal questions are different, and treating them as one case would misstate both.

QuestionSITLA parcel (this story)Temple parcel (Van Dusen)
OwnerState trust-land systemThe Church, privately, before the project began
State-property exemptionPotentially appliesNot relevant — never state land

Tool usedProposed rezone / development agreementLegislative development agreement (Ordinance 23-16)
County resultRejected, Aug. 2026Approved
Live litigationNone identifiedYes — merits pending at the Utah Supreme Court

What Van Dusen actually tests is whether Wasatch County's use of a "legislative development agreement" — a tool to approve a project that departs from existing zoning — was lawful on ordinary, privately owned land. The Utah Supreme Court lifted a construction injunction in February 2026 without ruling on that underlying question; oral argument on the merits is calendared for September 2026. It's a real, worth-tracking test of a tool Wasatch County has also used on the SITLA parcel's own negotiations — but it does not decide anything about SITLA's zoning exemption.

06Beyond Wasatch County

Three counties, three different answers

The most useful thing about the Wasatch parcel is that it isn't unique. The same exemption shows up in at least two other Utah counties, and SITLA handles it differently each time.

CountyWhat happenedSITLA's posture
Wayne (2016) Gravel pit proposed on SITLA land; county was advised it lacked zoning jurisdiction and formally declined to act. Exemption asserted, county deferred without a vote
Millard (Jan. 2026) County approved rezoning 4,400+ acres, much of it SITLA land, for a solar farm tied to AI data-center development, over public opposition on water use. Ordinary county process, no jurisdiction dispute
Wasatch (2004–2026) SITLA sought county rezoning twice, was denied twice, negotiated a development agreement, and was rejected a third time in Aug. 2026 — despite a 2024 public threat to bypass the county entirely. Negotiated for two decades; exemption threatened, not yet used

Read together, these three counties show that SITLA's own practice varies by parcel and by moment — it may invoke the exemption, negotiate voluntarily, withdraw after local opposition, or run the ordinary county process start to finish. The statute doesn't predict which one happens next; SITLA's own institutional revenue incentives, and how loudly a county pushes back, appear to matter more.

07The open question

Where this actually stands

SITLA is legally independent and owes its loyalty to trust beneficiaries — mainly public schools — not to the counties its land sits inside. Its board is appointed by the governor and confirmed by the Senate. Its powers are written by the Legislature. And state law appears to put real limits on how much say a county gets over what happens on SITLA land.

On this specific parcel, SITLA has spent twenty-two years choosing negotiation over its own legal leverage. The unanswered question, after the county's third rejection, is whether that choice holds — or whether the exemption a SITLA official has already publicly threatened to use finally gets used.
