What is this about?

Two sets of rules decide how much your vote counts in Utah:

  • The map. It draws the lines for Utah's four seats in the U.S. House.
  • The primary rules. They decide who can vote to pick each party's candidates.

Both changed between 2018 and 2026.

What happened to the map?

In 2018, voters passed Prop 4. It set up a commission to draw maps and banned maps drawn to help one party. Lawmakers could still pick their own map, but only after voting on the commission's maps, hearing from the public and explaining why in writing.

In 2020, lawmakers changed Prop 4. In 2021, they passed their own map. It split Salt Lake County four ways. Republicans won all four House seats in 2022 and 2024.

Voters' groups sued. Courts ruled against how lawmakers handled Prop 4. In 2025, a judge picked a new map. In November 2026, Democrat Ben McAdams won the new District 1. The count becomes official Nov. 23.

What happened to the primary rules?

Each party decides who can vote in its primary. The Republican primary is for Republicans only.

In 2021, lawmakers set a deadline for switching from one party to another before a primary. The sponsor said Democrats were switching to sway Republican races. A Princeton study found most new Republicans were new or unaffiliated voters instead.

Registrations shifted after the law. We found no study showing it changed who won.

Who paid?

  • The drive to repeal Prop 4 got more than $4.3 million from one out-of-state nonprofit. Its donors are not public.
  • The group against Amendment B raised about $1.28 million. About 44% of its 2026 cash came from out of state.
  • Taxpayers paid the Legislature's lawyers just over $1.859 million on the map case, as a reporter found. No running total is posted.

Both sides used money that passed through nonprofits first, so voters can't see where all of it started.
