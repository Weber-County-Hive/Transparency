The Amendments on Your Ballot, and How We Got Here

Two changes to the Utah Constitution are on the Nov. 3, 2026 ballot. Both affect how citizens and voters take part in lawmaking. Here is what each one says, what a YES or NO vote does, the history that led to them, and what people are saying, checked against the record.

Built from the 2026 proposed constitutional amendments (le.utah.gov), the Davis County 2026 General Election Notice, court rulings and news records. Commenters are identified by initials only. Companion page: Amendment B: Myth vs. Fact.

Start here

Amendment A changes how the public is told about proposed constitutional amendments. Today the Constitution says they must be printed in a newspaper in every county for two months. Amendment A removes the newspaper rule and lets the Legislature decide the method by statute, for the 60 days before the election.

Amendment B says a statewide citizen initiative that includes a new tax, a tax on more items, or a higher rate needs at least 60% of the vote instead of a simple majority.

Both were written and passed by the Legislature. Neither one changes how the Legislature itself passes laws or taxes.

The very simple version: Amendment A is about the notice: how you find out what is on the ballot. Amendment B is about the score: how many votes citizens need to win when their idea includes a tax. In 2024, the courts threw out two amendments because the notice rule wasn't followed. Amendment A changes that notice rule.

Why it is on the ballot

In 2024, courts voided two amendments because the newspaper notice was not published:

  • 2024 Amendment D would have let the Legislature amend or repeal any citizen initiative. The Utah Supreme Court voided it (ruling Sept. 25, 2024; formal opinion Oct. 24, 2024) for two reasons: the newspaper notice was not published, and the ballot language was misleading. Then-Justice Diana Hagen: "We simply cannot read the word 'newspaper' out of the Publication Clause." (FOX 13; KUER)
  • 2024 Amendment A would have removed the requirement that income tax be used only for education and services for children and people with disabilities. District Judge Laura Scott voided it on Oct. 9, 2024 for the same publication failure. It stayed on ballots, but votes were not counted. (KUER)

The court described the rule this way: "The submission and publication requirements are not mere boxes to be checked before votes can be tallied; they are constitutional safeguards designed to ensure that voters have the information and time necessary to cast an informed vote." (Ballotpedia, March 12, 2025)

What it changes and what it doesn't

  • Changes: the newspaper requirement leaves the Constitution, and the notice method becomes something the Legislature sets and can later change by ordinary bill.
  • Doesn't change: amendments still need two-thirds of each house to reach the ballot and a majority of voters to pass.
  • The statute that would set the new method (found Oct. 7, 2026): H.B. 481 (2025, Loubet/McCay). If A passes, the Lt. Governor publishes the full text of each proposed amendment as a "class A notice" for the 60 days before the election. It takes effect Jan. 1, 2027, only if A passes. See Where the notice would run.

Amendment B: 60% for citizen tax initiatives

S.J.R. 2 (2025). Primary sponsor: Sen. Lincoln Fillmore. House sponsor: Rep. Jason B. Kyle. Passed the Senate 21–8 (Feb. 5, 2025) and the House 55–17 (March 6, 2025).

An initiative needs at least 60% if it "includes" (A) a new tax, (B) an existing tax expanded to more items or transactions, (C) a rate increase, or (D) a property-tax rate that decreases less than current law would. It applies to votes after Nov. 1, 2026, and only to statewide citizen initiatives.

The question on your ballot:

"Shall the Utah Constitution be amended to require that citizen-proposed laws that increase taxes compared to existing law be approved by at least 60% of voters, instead of a simple majority?"

Ballot text as printed in the Davis County 2026 General Election Notice. Note the ballot says "increase taxes"; the constitutional text says "includes."

Part D note: under Truth in Taxation the certified property-tax rate falls as values rise, so Part D reaches initiatives that keep revenue higher than the falling rate would, not ordinary tax cuts.

OPEN QUESTION

Who decides whether an initiative "includes" a tax? S.J.R. 2 does not say, and no record reviewed for this page answers it. "Current law" in Part D is also undefined. Both questions go to the sponsors (see Questions to ask).

How and when you are told

  • Feb.–March 2025: The Legislature passes H.J.R. 10 (A) and S.J.R. 2 (B), about 20 months before the vote (Ballotpedia).
  • Today's rule: Proposed amendments must be published "in at least one newspaper in every county… for two months" before the election (Utah Const. Art. XXIII, Sec. 1).
  • If A passes: Notice runs "for 60 calendar days immediately preceding the next general election," by a method the Legislature sets by statute.
  • Voter information pamphlet: Arguments for and against each amendment are published by the Lt. Governor's office at vote.utah.gov.
  • Nov. 3, 2026: General election.

In black and white

YES on A

  • Newspaper notice is no longer required by the Constitution.
  • The Legislature sets the notice method by statute.
  • Notice runs the 60 days before the election.

NO on A

  • The Constitution keeps requiring newspaper notice in every county for two months.
  • Changing the method later still takes a constitutional amendment.

YES on B

  • A citizen initiative that includes a tax increase, as S.J.R. 2 defines it, needs 60%: a new tax, a tax on more items, a higher rate, or a property-tax rate that falls less than current law would. Plain tax cuts still need a simple majority.
  • Initiatives with no tax still need a simple majority.
  • Lawmakers, counties, cities and districts are unchanged.

NO on B

  • Every statewide citizen initiative keeps the simple-majority rule.
  • The existing checks stay: legal review, cost estimate, 7 hearings, ~140,000 signatures from 26 of 29 Senate districts.
  • The Legislature can still amend initiatives afterward. Since July 2024, a change that impairs an initiative that reforms government must be narrowly tailored to a compelling government interest (Utah Supreme Court).

Sources: le.utah.gov, 2026 proposed constitutional amendments; S.J.R. 2 (2025); vote.utah.gov, "Instructions for a Statewide Initiative"; League of Women Voters v. Utah Legislature, Utah Supreme Court, July 11, 2024.

Who Amendment B could help, and who it could hurt

Effects of how the rule works, not anyone's motives. Only the two named groups have taken a public position.

Could help

  • Anyone opposing a new tax: 40% + 1 now blocks a citizen tax initiative
  • Businesses with tax breaks or exemptions (closing a break = 60%), e.g. data centers at 0.5% vs. 6%
  • The Legislature: keeps more control over tax policy
  • Well-funded campaigns against an initiative
  • On record, supports: Utah Taxpayers Association; Taxpayer Shield PAC, chaired by S.J.R. 2 sponsor Sen. Lincoln Fillmore (Axios, Oct. 7, 2026; state PAC filing)

Could hurt

  • Initiatives that fund a program with a tax: health care (Prop 3), schools, water and the Great Salt Lake, parks, transit, housing
  • People those programs serve
  • "Fair share" and loophole-closing measures, even when most residents' taxes go down
  • Small, volunteer initiative campaigns
  • On record, opposes: Utahns Protecting Our Constitution

Not affected

  • The Legislature, county commissions, city councils, school boards, PID, MIDA and UIPA boards
  • Initiatives with no tax, and initiatives that only cut a tax

Sources: S.J.R. 2 (2025); KSL, Jan. 30, 2025 (Utah Taxpayers Association); state campaign filings (Utahns Protecting Our Constitution); MIDA–Box Elder interlocal §6(a); Ballotpedia (Prop 3).

Tap a year for the record

2018: Voters pass Props 2, 3 and 4

Prop 2 (medical cannabis) and Prop 3 (Medicaid, 0.15% sales tax) each passed with 53%. Prop 4 created an independent redistricting commission. (NORML; Ballotpedia; KJZZ)

Dec. 2018 – 2020: Lawmakers change all three

H.B. 3001 replaced Prop 2 and removed home growing (Dec. 3, 2018). S.B. 96 changed Prop 3 (signed Feb. 11, 2019). S.B. 200 replaced Prop 4 (2020).

Dec. 2019 – Jan. 2020: A citizen referendum stops a tax increase

S.B. 2001 raised the sales tax on food. Citizens filed 152,000 referendum signatures; lawmakers repealed it Jan. 27, 2020. (Ballotpedia)

July 11, 2024: Supreme Court rules on Prop 4

Unanimous: reform initiatives are protected from unlimited repeal. Justice Paige Petersen: "When Utahns exercise their right to reform the government through a citizen initiative, their exercise of these rights is protected from government infringement." (KJZZ)

Newspaper notice was not published as the Constitution requires. Amendment D's ballot language was also found misleading. (FOX 13; KUER)

Feb.–March 2025: A and B referred to the 2026 ballot

H.J.R. 10 passed 70–0 and 29–0. S.J.R. 2 passed 21–8 and 55–17. (Ballotpedia)

Aug. 2025: Prop 4 repeal ruled unconstitutional

A district court struck the 2021 congressional map. (Ballotpedia, Aug. 29, 2025)

What voters passed vs. what the Legislature changed

Each 2018 initiative: what the law looked like before, what voters approved, and what happened after.

Prop 2: medical cannabis

Passed with 53% · No new tax

Before
2014 law let epilepsy patients possess certain CBD products, but "provided no legal in-state supply source" (NORML). In 2018, H.B. 195 allowed cannabis products for patients with "six months or less to live"; H.B. 197 (state growing) first failed 36–34 because it "needed 38 votes" (KSL), then passed later (NORML).
Voters passed
Home growing (6 plants) for patients over 100 miles from a dispensary; at least one dispensary per county; edibles; autoimmune disorders and "chronic or debilitating pain" as qualifying conditions. Fees were to cover about $1.4 million of ongoing costs; medical cannabis was exempt from sales tax (FOX 13, citing the Governor's budget office).
Legislature changed (H.B. 3001, Dec. 3, 2018)
No home growing; 5 licensed "pharmacies" with a pharmacist, plus a state central fill pharmacy; no edibles; flower only in blister packs; autoimmune disorders dropped and pain must last over two weeks without responding to conventional medicine; cities may zone pharmacies out of some areas (KSL; NORML).
Today
"Medical cannabis products are exempt from taxes, including the state sales tax" (Marijuana Policy Project).

Prop 3: Medicaid expansion

Passed with about 53% · 0.15% statewide sales tax

Before
2015: the House voted down Gov. Herbert's "Healthy Utah" plan. 2016: H.B. 437 covered "a little more than sixteen thousand Utahns" in extreme poverty (KUER, March 8, 2016).
Voters passed
Coverage up to 138% of the poverty level; federal government pays 90%, Utah 10%; a 0.15% sales tax for Utah's share.
Legislature changed (S.B. 96, Feb. 11, 2019)
Coverage cut to 100% of the poverty level from April 1, 2019; Utah's share under that plan 30%, not 10% (Jurist); the full 0.15% tax kept (Ballotpedia). Fiscal analysts estimated a "two-year cost of $49 million," about five times Prop 3's projected $10.4 million shortfall (Salt Lake Tribune, Feb. 4, 2019), while giving up $103 million (FY2019) and $407 million (FY2020) in federal funds (S.B. 96 fiscal note).
After
The federal government rejected the cap and would not pay 90% below 138% (Deseret News, Aug. 22, 2019). Full expansion began January 2020 with a work requirement, suspended April 2020 and later removed (healthinsurance.org). H.B. 15 (2026) ends expansion sooner if the federal share drops below 90% (le.utah.gov).

Prop 4: independent redistricting

Passed with 50.34% · No tax

Voters passed
An independent commission to draw maps and submit them to the Legislature; a ban on partisan gerrymandering.
Legislature changed (S.B. 200, 2020)
The commission became advisory, so the Legislature can ignore its maps; the partisan-gerrymandering ban was repealed (League of Women Voters).
After
July 2024: Utah Supreme Court protects government-reform initiatives from unlimited changes. Aug. 2025: district court rules the repeal unconstitutional and strikes the 2021 map (Ballotpedia). 2026: a Utah GOP-led initiative to repeal Prop 4 failed to qualify after signature withdrawals in Senate District 15 (Ballotpedia, March 28, 2026).

If Amendment B had applied in 2018, only Prop 3 would have needed 60%. B applies to votes after Nov. 1, 2026 and does not reach back.

Fact or fiction?

Claims we keep seeing in the comments. Pick an answer, then see the record.

New: Does Amendment B do what you think? Take the 12-question quiz →

1Amendment B applies to every citizen initiative.

Fiction

Only statewide citizen initiatives that include a tax increase, as S.J.R. 2 defines it. Other initiatives, tax-cut-only initiatives and referendums still need a simple majority.

2If B passes, the Legislature will also need 60% to raise taxes.

Fiction

B changes nothing for lawmakers. Bills still pass with "the majority of all the members elected to each house" (Utah Const. Art. VI, Sec. 22): 38 of 75 and 15 of 29.

3The Legislature needs a majority of all members, not just those present, to pass a bill.

Fact

Art. VI, Sec. 22. In 2018 a cannabis bill failed 36–34 because it "needed 38 votes, a constitutional majority" (KSL).

4A statewide initiative can be put on a low-turnout, odd-year ballot.

Fiction

It "must be submitted to voters at a regular, general election" (Utah Code 20A-7-203, -208), held in November of even-numbered years (20A-1-102).

5The governor can veto a law passed by initiative.

Fiction

"The governor may not veto a law adopted by the people" (Utah Code 20A-7-212). The Legislature can amend it, though, by its normal majority.

6The Legislature changed all three initiatives voters passed in 2018.

Fact

Prop 2 by H.B. 3001 (Dec. 3, 2018), Prop 3 by S.B. 96 (Feb. 11, 2019), Prop 4 by S.B. 200 (2020).

7Prop 2, medical cannabis, raised taxes.

Fiction

Fees were to cover most costs, and medical cannabis was made exempt from sales tax (Governor's budget office fiscal statement, via FOX 13). It is still tax-exempt (Marijuana Policy Project).

8The Legislature kept Prop 3's full sales tax while cutting its coverage.

Fact

S.B. 96 kept the 0.15% tax (Ballotpedia) and cut coverage from 138% to 100% of the poverty level until January 2020.

9The Legislature's Medicaid rewrite was projected to cost the state more at first.

Fact

Fiscal analysts estimated a "two-year cost of $49 million," about five times Prop 3's projected $10.4 million shortfall (Salt Lake Tribune, Feb. 4, 2019).

10Voting yes on B will undo the 2018 initiatives.

Fiction

B applies only to votes "after November 1, 2026" (S.J.R. 2 text). It doesn't reach back.

11No state requires voters to pass anything by a supermajority.

Fiction

Arizona's Prop 132 (2022) requires 60% for initiatives that create a new tax (AZFamily). Florida has required 60% for constitutional amendments since 2006 (Axios).

12Salt Lake County alone can pass a statewide initiative.

Fiction

It has about 34.5% of Utah's people (USAFacts / U.S. Census, 2025). Initiatives also need signatures in 26 of 29 Senate districts.

13Utah once required initiative signatures from 20 of its 29 counties.

Fact

The Utah Supreme Court struck that rule down in Gallivan v. Walker (2002 UT 89) for diluting urban voters' power.

14Only Democrats voted against Amendment B in the Legislature.

Fiction

The no votes included 2 Republican senators and 4 Republican representatives, plus every Democrat (Ballotpedia, March 10, 2025).

15A majority of voters could vote to take your house.

Fiction

Utah's Constitution bars it (Art. I, Sec. 22). Government can take property through eminent domain, for public use with just compensation, with no public vote (Utah Code 78B-6-501).

16Utah's Supreme Court grew from 5 to 7 justices in 2026.

Fact

S.B. 134, signed January 2026 (Ballotpedia), over objections from the chief justice and the Utah State Bar (Salt Lake Tribune).

17Tax initiatives just let voters raise taxes on 'other people.'

Partly true

A statewide initiative applies to everyone who votes for it. Yes voters tax themselves too. Prop 3's sales tax is paid by every shopper, including the people who voted for it. (Prop 3 (2018); Ballotpedia)

18Amendment B lets the majority decide.

Fiction

Under B, a tax initiative needs 60%. That means 41% of voters can block the other 59%. Without B, a simple majority decides. (S.J.R. 2 (2025))

19Once voters pass an initiative, it's final.

Fiction

The Legislature can amend initiatives after they pass. It changed Prop 2 within weeks and Prop 3 within three months. Since 2024, changes to government-reform initiatives are limited by the courts. (H.B. 3001 (2018); S.B. 96 (2019); League of Women Voters v. Utah Legislature (2024))

20Citizens keep raising taxes by initiative.

Fiction

Utah voters have approved 7 citizen initiatives, ever. Only 1 raised a tax: Prop 3 (2018). The Legislature kept that tax. Since 2015 alone, the Legislature has passed at least 9 tax increases or new taxing powers. (Ballotpedia Historical Ballot Measures Factbook; FOX 13; le.utah.gov)

Taxes and tax tools the Legislature created

Amendment B sets a 60% bar for citizen initiatives. These are the taxes and tax tools the Legislature itself created or expanded. Each one passed by a simple majority of each house, and in none of them do voters approve the final tax or zone.

Some of these tools come with checks. Some need a review committee or a participation agreement with the affected taxing entities. HOPZ needs a public notice. Many are bonded for decades. Each card says what the record shows.

The short version

TIF (tax increment financing) is the tool most of these share. The value of an area is frozen at a "base year," and the new property or sales tax collected as the area grows goes to the project instead of to schools, the county, cities and special districts for up to 25 to 45 years. A PID is different: it is its own government that bills property owners inside it directly.

The collective questions

  • Power: A moves the notice method from the Constitution to statute, where the Legislature can change it by majority. B raises the vote citizens need for tax initiatives only.
  • Transparency: A ends the newspaper requirement that voided two 2024 amendments; how notice will be given instead is set later by statute. B's trigger is "includes" and "current law" is undefined.
  • Financial accountability: Neither amendment changes how the Legislature, local governments, MIDA, UIPA or PID boards raise taxes or issue debt.
  • Environmental impact: No direct effect; initiatives that fund resource protection with a tax would face B's 60% bar.
  • Community impact: Both change how residents take part in constitutional and initiative decisions; neither touches decisions made without a public vote.

Every tool, and who gets to use it

Tools voters and citizens have

  • Statewide initiative: write a new law and put it to a vote. The only tool Amendment B changes.
  • Statewide referendum: challenge a law the Legislature just passed. Majority vote.
  • Local initiative and referendum: the same tools for city and county laws, with signature counts based on local voters. Amendment B covers statewide initiatives only.
  • Voting on constitutional amendments: every amendment, including A and B, needs voter approval.
  • Bond elections: general obligation bonds go to voters. Lease revenue bonds and PID bonds do not.
  • Truth in Taxation hearings: residents can speak before a property-tax increase. It's a hearing, not a vote.

Tools the Legislature and local governments have

  • Pass a bill: majority of each house, including tax increases.
  • Override a veto: two-thirds of each house.
  • Propose a constitutional amendment: two-thirds of each house, then voters decide.
  • Call itself into special session: two-thirds of each house for a "persistent fiscal crisis, war, natural disaster, or emergency in the affairs of the state" (2018 amendment; used Aug. 2024).
  • Amend or repeal an initiative afterward: by majority, with the 2024 limit for government-reform initiatives.
  • Repeal its own law before a referendum vote: as with the 2019 tax law in January 2020.
  • Local governments: raise property tax after a Truth in Taxation hearing, adopt some sales taxes by council or commission vote, and create PIDs, CRAs and other zones (see Tax tools).

Sources: vote.utah.gov; Utah Const. Art. VII Sec. 8, Art. XXIII; KUER, April 22, 2025, and Utah News Dispatch, Aug. 2024, on special sessions; CASE 2026-STAX-01 (Weber County 0.20% sales tax).
