The Amendments on Your Ballot, and How We Got Here

Two changes to the Utah Constitution are on the Nov. 3, 2026 ballot. Both affect how citizens and voters take part in lawmaking. Here is what each one says, what a YES or NO vote does, the history that led to them, and what people are saying, checked against the record.

Built from the 2026 proposed constitutional amendments (le.utah.gov), the Davis County 2026 General Election Notice, court rulings and news records. Commenters are identified by initials only. Companion page: Amendment B: Myth vs. Fact.

Start here

Amendment A changes how the public is told about proposed constitutional amendments. Today the Constitution says they must be printed in a newspaper in every county for two months. Amendment A removes the newspaper rule and lets the Legislature decide the method by statute, for the 60 days before the election.

Amendment B says a statewide citizen initiative that includes a new tax, a tax on more items, or a higher rate needs at least 60% of the vote instead of a simple majority.

Both were written and passed by the Legislature. Neither one changes how the Legislature itself passes laws or taxes.

The very simple version: Amendment A is about the notice: how you find out what is on the ballot. Amendment B is about the score: how many votes citizens need to win when their idea includes a tax. In 2024, the courts threw out two amendments because the notice rule wasn't followed. Amendment A changes that notice rule.

Amendment A: How you are told about amendments

Why it is on the ballot

In 2024, courts voided two amendments because the newspaper notice was not published:

  • 2024 Amendment D would have let the Legislature amend or repeal any citizen initiative. The Utah Supreme Court voided it (ruling Sept. 25, 2024; formal opinion Oct. 24, 2024) for two reasons: the newspaper notice was not published, and the ballot language was misleading. Justice Diana Hagen: "We simply cannot read the word 'newspaper' out of the Publication Clause." (FOX 13; KUER)
  • 2024 Amendment A would have removed the requirement that income tax be used only for education and services for children and people with disabilities. District Judge Laura Scott voided it on Oct. 9, 2024 for the same publication failure. It stayed on ballots, but votes were not counted. (KUER)

The court described the rule this way: "The submission and publication requirements are not mere boxes to be checked before votes can be tallied; they are constitutional safeguards designed to ensure that voters have the information and time necessary to cast an informed vote." (Ballotpedia, March 12, 2025)

What it changes and what it doesn't

  • Changes: the newspaper requirement leaves the Constitution, and the notice method becomes something the Legislature sets and can later change by ordinary bill.
  • Doesn't change: amendments still need two-thirds of each house to reach the ballot and a majority of voters to pass.
  • Not yet known from the record here: the specific statute that would set the new method. Check le.utah.gov before relying on any description of it.

Amendment B: 60% for citizen tax initiatives

S.J.R. 2 (2025). Primary sponsor: Sen. Lincoln Fillmore. House sponsor: Rep. Jason B. Kyle. Passed the Senate 21–8 (Feb. 5, 2025) and the House 55–17 (March 6, 2025).

An initiative needs at least 60% if it "includes" (A) a new tax, (B) an existing tax expanded to more items or transactions, (C) a rate increase, or (D) a property-tax rate that decreases less than current law would. It applies to votes after Nov. 1, 2026, and only to statewide citizen initiatives.

Part D note: under Truth in Taxation the certified property-tax rate falls as values rise, so Part D reaches initiatives that keep revenue higher than the falling rate would, not ordinary tax cuts.

In black and white

YES on A

  • Newspaper notice is no longer required by the Constitution.
  • The Legislature sets the notice method by statute.
  • Notice runs the 60 days before the election.

NO on A

  • The Constitution keeps requiring newspaper notice in every county for two months.
  • Changing the method later still takes a constitutional amendment.

YES on B

  • A citizen initiative that includes a tax change needs 60%.
  • Initiatives with no tax still need a simple majority.
  • Lawmakers, counties, cities and districts are unchanged.

NO on B

  • Every statewide citizen initiative keeps the simple-majority rule.
  • The existing checks stay: legal review, cost estimate, 7 hearings, ~140,000 signatures from 26 of 29 Senate districts.
  • The Legislature can still amend any initiative afterward.

Sources: le.utah.gov, 2026 proposed constitutional amendments; vote.utah.gov, "Instructions for a Statewide Initiative."

The citizen side of the ledger

  • 2019: The Legislature passed S.B. 2001 (Dec. 12, 2019), raising the sales tax on food. Citizens turned in 152,000 referendum signatures, and the Legislature repealed the law on Jan. 27, 2020. (Ballotpedia)
  • Utah Const. Art. I, Sec. 2: "All political power is inherent in the people; and all free governments are founded on their authority for their equal protection and benefit, and they have the right to alter or reform their government as the public welfare may require." (Justia)

Comments and responses, checked

Claims from public Facebook debates in October 2026. Commenters are identified by initials only, and only when a name was available. Where a response was posted, its record-based points are summarized.

B.W. · post
"In 2018 we had three [initiatives], and voters passed all three… The Legislature subsequently changed all three of them."
Verified

Prop 2 was replaced by H.B. 3001 (Dec. 3, 2018), Prop 3 changed by S.B. 96 (Feb. 11, 2019), and Prop 4 replaced by S.B. 200 (2020). (NORML; Ballotpedia; KJZZ)

B.W. · post
"Proposition 3 passed with 53.3% of the vote. Under the proposed 60% rule, an initiative like that could win a majority… and still lose."
Verified

Prop 3 included a 0.15% sales tax and passed with 53% (Ballotpedia; Salt Lake Tribune, Nov. 7, 2018). Because it included a tax, it would have needed 60% under S.J.R. 2.

B.W. · post
"The Utah Supreme Court… said… when citizens use an initiative to reform their government, the Legislature does not have unlimited power to simply undo that reform."
Verified

Unanimous ruling, July 11, 2024. Justice Paige Petersen: "When Utahns exercise their right to reform the government through a citizen initiative, their exercise of these rights is protected from government infringement." (KJZZ)

B.W. · post
"Amendment D… was ultimately blocked from taking effect because constitutional requirements had not been properly followed."
Verified

Voided for failure to publish newspaper notice, and the court also found the ballot language misleading (FOX 13, Oct. 24, 2024). That newspaper rule is what Amendment A would change.

B.W. · post
"…a project near Price involving hundreds of small nuclear reactors."
Unverified

No primary record reviewed for this page. Not used as a finding.

A.B. · comment
"If a ballot initiative has an element that could be considered a tax, it will need the higher threshold… All the recent ballot initiatives that have passed have elements that could be argued to contain a tax."
Partly true

Supported: S.J.R. 2 uses "includes," so one tax element puts the whole initiative at 60%.

Not supported: "all recent initiatives." Prop 4 (2018, redistricting) had no tax. Non-tax initiatives keep the simple majority.

S.M. · response
"Really everything is tied to a tax, so if they put that word in at all, it changes it."
Verified (as to funding)

Every statewide initiative gets an official cost estimate (vote.utah.gov). If citizens include a tax to pay for their own initiative, it needs 60% (S.J.R. 2). If they leave the funding out, the Legislature decides whether to fund it. Prop 3 funded itself with a sales tax.

Commenter · comment
"Two things can be true. One can oppose MIDA and PIDs… and also support Amendment B. Two separate issues."
Partly true

They are separate votes. The link: B does not change MIDA, UIPA or PID decisions, which had no public vote, but a citizen initiative making data centers pay the standard 6% energy tax instead of 0.5% (MIDA–Box Elder interlocal, §6(a)) would need 60%.

Commenter · comment
"Any increase or any new tax should have a higher margin to be passed. This amendment protects YOUR wallet."
Partly true

B adds a higher margin only for citizen initiatives. Increases by the Legislature (H.B. 362, 2015 fuel tax; S.B. 2001, 2019 food tax), counties (Weber County 7.25%, 2024), districts and PID boards keep their current rules.

Commenter · comment
"The only people who would vote no on Amendment B are the people who want to raise my taxes."
Not supported

A NO vote keeps the current rule; it does not raise any tax. In 2019–20 it was citizens' referendum signatures (152,000) that led to repeal of a legislative food-tax increase (Ballotpedia).

News page · post
"Traditional bills face months of strict legislative vetting, while citizen initiatives bypass this policy-filtering process."
Partly true

Initiatives don't go through committees, but they do go through a legal review, an official cost estimate, 7 public hearings and ~140,000 verified signatures, and the Legislature can amend them afterward (vote.utah.gov; S.B. 96).

News page · post
"Lawmakers only need a simple majority to raise taxes… Medicaid expansion would have failed under this rule."
Verified

Both statements match the record (Utah Const. Art. VI; Ballotpedia on Prop 3).

J.K. · comment
Part D does not reach ordinary tax cuts, because the Truth in Taxation certified rate falls as values rise.
Verified correction

Accepted. Earlier Hive graphics that described Part D as covering "a tax cut" are being corrected. The undefined phrase "current law" remains an open question.

Commenter · comment
"Wildlife initiatives already need 60%."
Partly true

The supermajority for wildlife initiatives exists, but it is two-thirds, not 60% (Utah Const. Art. VI).

Commenter · comment
"The Tax Commission denied 39 tax increases last year."
Partly true

Reported figure: 35 entities, $49.87M denied in 2025 under S.B. 29 / S.B. 202 rules. Those were process denials, not public votes.

Questions to ask, and whom to ask

  • Rep. Anthony Loubet (Amendment A)
    If A passes, what statute will set the notice method, and can a future Legislature change it without a statewide vote?
  • Lt. Governor's office (elections)
    How was notice of Amendments A and B published for 2026, and where can voters see it?
  • Sen. Fillmore and Rep. Kyle (Amendment B)
    Who decides whether an initiative "includes" a tax? When is "current law" measured for Part D?
  • Your state senator and representative
    Would you support a 60% bar for tax increases passed by the Legislature, or a public vote before PID bonds?
  • Your county commission and city council
    When did you last raise a tax, and was there a public vote?

The collective questions

  • Power: A moves the notice method from the Constitution to statute, where the Legislature can change it by majority. B raises the vote citizens need for tax initiatives only.
  • Transparency: A ends the newspaper requirement that voided two 2024 amendments; how notice will be given instead is set later by statute. B's trigger is "includes" and "current law" is undefined.
  • Financial accountability: Neither amendment changes how the Legislature, local governments, MIDA, UIPA or PID boards raise taxes or issue debt.
  • Environmental impact: No direct effect; initiatives that fund resource protection with a tax would face B's 60% bar.
  • Community impact: Both change how residents take part in constitutional and initiative decisions; neither touches decisions made without a public vote.

Words to know

Publication Clause

The part of Article XXIII, Sec. 1 that says how proposed amendments must be published before the vote. Amendment A rewrites it.

Statute

An ordinary law passed by the Legislature and signed by the governor. Easier to change than the Constitution.

Initiative

A law proposed by citizens and adopted by voters.

Referendum

A citizen challenge to a law already passed, put to voters by petition.

Voided

Ruled invalid by a court, so the votes do not take effect.

PID

Public Infrastructure District: a district that can levy assessments and issue bonds inside its boundary.
