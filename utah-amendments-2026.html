The Amendments on Your Ballot, and How We Got Here

Part 1 of 4: Amendment A, and how you are told

CASE 2026-AMD-01 · Posted Oct. 7, 2026 · Updated Oct. 17, 2026

Two changes to the Utah Constitution are on the Nov. 3, 2026 ballot. Both affect how citizens and voters take part in lawmaking. Here is what each one says, what a YES or NO vote does, the history that led to them, and what people are saying, checked against the record.

Start here

Amendment A changes how the public is told about proposed constitutional amendments. Today the Constitution says they must be printed in a newspaper in every county for two months. Amendment A removes the newspaper rule and lets the Legislature decide the method by statute, for the 60 days before the election.

Amendment B says a statewide citizen initiative that includes a new tax, a tax on more items, or a higher rate needs at least 60% of the vote instead of a simple majority.

Both were written and passed by the Legislature. Neither one changes how the Legislature itself passes laws or taxes.

The very simple version: Amendment A is about the notice: how you find out what is on the ballot. Amendment B is about the score: how many votes citizens need to win when their idea includes a tax. In 2024, the courts threw out two amendments because the notice rule wasn't followed. Amendment A changes that notice rule.

Five quick points (Part 1):

  • Two changes to the Utah Constitution are on the Nov. 3, 2026 ballot, Amendments A and B. Both were written and passed by the Legislature, and each needs a simple majority of voters.
  • Amendment A (H.J.R. 10, 2025) removes the rule that proposed amendments be printed in a newspaper in every county for two months. The Legislature would set the method by statute, for the 60 days before the election.
  • If A passes, H.B. 481 (2025) sets that method: a class A notice on the Utah Public Notice Website, the Lt. Governor's website and a public posting. It takes effect Jan. 1, 2027.
  • A is on the ballot because courts voided two 2024 amendments after the newspaper notice was not published.
  • A passed the House 70–0 and the Senate 29–0. Amendment B is on Part 2.

Amendment A: How you are told about amendments

The question on your ballot:

"Shall the Utah Constitution be amended to change how proposed constitutional amendments are published, requiring publication for 60 days immediately before the general election in a manner established by state law instead of in newspapers?"

Ballot text as printed in the Davis County 2026 General Election Notice.

H.J.R. 10 (2025). Primary sponsor: Rep. Anthony Loubet. Passed the House 70–0 (Feb. 26, 2025) and the Senate 29–0 (March 7, 2025).

Art. XXIII, Sec. 1TodayIf A passes
How notice is given"in at least one newspaper in every county of the state, where a newspaper is published""in a manner provided by statute"
How long"for two months""for 60 calendar days immediately preceding the next general election"
Who decides the methodThe Constitution (changing it takes two-thirds of each house plus a statewide vote)The Legislature, by statute (a simple majority of each house and the governor)

Text as reported by Ballotpedia, "Utah Publication Requirements for Constitutional Amendments Measure (2026)"; full text at le.utah.gov, 2026 proposed constitutional amendments.

Why it is on the ballot

In 2024, courts voided two amendments because the newspaper notice was not published:

  • 2024 Amendment D would have let the Legislature amend or repeal any citizen initiative. The Utah Supreme Court voided it (ruling Sept. 25, 2024; formal opinion Oct. 24, 2024) for two reasons: the newspaper notice was not published, and the ballot language was misleading. Then-Justice Diana Hagen: "We simply cannot read the word 'newspaper' out of the Publication Clause." (FOX 13; KUER)
  • 2024 Amendment A would have removed the requirement that income tax be used only for education and services for children and people with disabilities. District Judge Laura Scott voided it on Oct. 9, 2024 for the same publication failure. It stayed on ballots, but votes were not counted. (KUER)

The court described the rule this way: "The submission and publication requirements are not mere boxes to be checked before votes can be tallied; they are constitutional safeguards designed to ensure that voters have the information and time necessary to cast an informed vote." (Ballotpedia, March 12, 2025)

What it changes and what it doesn't

  • Changes: the newspaper requirement leaves the Constitution, and the notice method becomes something the Legislature sets and can later change by ordinary bill.
  • Doesn't change: amendments still need two-thirds of each house to reach the ballot and a majority of voters to pass.
  • The statute that would set the new method (found Oct. 7, 2026): H.B. 481 (2025, Loubet/McCay). If A passes, the Lt. Governor publishes the full text of each proposed amendment as a "class A notice" for the 60 days before the election. It takes effect Jan. 1, 2027, only if A passes. See Where the notice would run.
  • Added Oct. 17, 2026, cost: The 2026 Voter Information Pamphlet's fiscal analysis says that with H.B. 481, the state could reduce costs "by up to $1.4 million every two years, based on recent estimates." The Salt Lake Tribune (Oct. 12, 2026) reports that a 2025 state law requires future ballot initiatives to meet the same publication rule, so if A fails, initiative backers would pay that publication cost. (Voter Information Pamphlet; Salt Lake Tribune)

Updates since this was posted

News and records found online after the Oct. 7, 2026 posted date, plus two earlier items that add context. Searched on Oct. 17, 2026 for: court actions, the official Voter Information Pamphlet, campaign finance for the yes and no committees, and polls reported by news outlets.

  • Sept. 8–12, 2026 (reported Sept. 28 and Oct. 6; added for context)A Noble Predictive Insights poll of 602 registered Utah voters found about 50% support Amendment B, 21% oppose and 23% unsure (margin of error ±3.99 points). This is the only public poll on the amendments found in searches on Oct. 17.Source: Utah News Dispatch, Sept. 28, 2026; Deseret News, Oct. 6, 2026
  • Sept. 28, 2026 (added for context)Utahns Protecting Our Constitution, a political issues committee led by Elizabeth Rasmussen of Better Boundaries, launched a "vote no" campaign on Amendment B. Utah News Dispatch reported the committee had collected about $593,000 in contributions as of September. The committee has not taken a position on Amendment A.Source: Utah News Dispatch, Sept. 28, 2026
  • September 2026 (found Oct. 17)The 2026 Voter Information Pamphlet is posted. Amendment A: argument for by Rep. Anthony Loubet and Sen. Daniel McCay; no argument against, because it passed unanimously. Amendment B: argument for by Sen. Lincoln Fillmore and Rep. Jason Kyle; argument against by Sen. Luz Escamilla and Rep. Angela Romero. Fiscal analysis: A with H.B. 481 could reduce state costs "by up to $1.4 million every two years"; B "likely would not materially impact current state revenues or expenditures."Source: 2026 Voter Information Pamphlet
  • Oct. 8, 2026The Deseret News ran opinion pieces on both sides of Amendment B: one by its sponsors, Sen. Fillmore and Rep. Kyle, urging a yes vote, and one by Karen Crompton and Dixie S. Huefner of the Utah Citizens' Counsel, urging a no vote.Source: Deseret News (yes); Deseret News (no)
  • Oct. 9, 2026FOX 13 reported campaigning on both sides of Amendment B: Americans for Prosperity Utah paid for mailers supporting it and planned digital ads; Better Boundaries launched a TV ad opposing it.Source: FOX 13, Oct. 9, 2026
  • Oct. 12, 2026The Salt Lake Tribune reported that both 2026 amendments had to be published in newspapers, at an estimated cost of $1.4 million, and that no organized opposition to Amendment A appears to exist. It also reported that a search of newspaper archives shows the publication rule "was not always met" in past years.Source: Salt Lake Tribune, Oct. 12, 2026
  • Oct. 13, 2026General-election ballots were scheduled to be mailed. The deadline to request a ballot is Oct. 27 at 5 p.m. Election Day is Nov. 3.Source: vote.utah.gov
  • Oct. 13, 2026KUER and Utah News Dispatch published explainers. Utah News Dispatch cites a Ballotpedia count: the Legislature has referred 192 measures to Utah's ballot since 1895, against 28 citizen initiatives, and referrals passed nearly 70% of the time against 25% for initiatives.Source: KUER, Oct. 13, 2026; Utah News Dispatch, Oct. 13, 2026

No news report of campaign finance filings by Taxpayer Shield PAC or Utahns Protecting Our Constitution dated after Oct. 7 was found, and the state disclosure site could not be read by the tools used for this update, so the money figures on Part 4 are unchanged.Source: news searches (Salt Lake Tribune, Deseret News, KSL, KUER, FOX 13, Utah News Dispatch, Axios)
