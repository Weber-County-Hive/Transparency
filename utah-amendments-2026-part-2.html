Short version
  • Amendment B (S.J.R. 2, 2025) requires 60% of the vote, instead of a simple majority, for a statewide citizen initiative that "includes" a new tax, a tax on more items, a higher rate, or a property-tax rate that falls less than current law would.
  • It does not change how the Legislature, counties, cities or districts raise taxes. The Legislature still passes taxes by a majority of each house.
  • It passed the Senate 21–8 and the House 55–17. All 76 yes votes were Republicans; 6 Republicans and 19 Democrats voted no.
  • Prop 3 (2018) passed with 53% and included a tax, so it would have needed 60% under B. B applies only to votes after Nov. 1, 2026.
  • Who decides whether an initiative "includes" a tax is not stated in S.J.R. 2.

Amendment B: 60% for citizen tax initiatives

S.J.R. 2 (2025). Primary sponsor: Sen. Lincoln Fillmore. House sponsor: Rep. Jason B. Kyle. Passed the Senate 21–8 (Feb. 5, 2025) and the House 55–17 (March 6, 2025).

An initiative needs at least 60% if it "includes" (A) a new tax, (B) an existing tax expanded to more items or transactions, (C) a rate increase, or (D) a property-tax rate that decreases less than current law would. It applies to votes after Nov. 1, 2026, and only to statewide citizen initiatives.

The question on your ballot:

"Shall the Utah Constitution be amended to require that citizen-proposed laws that increase taxes compared to existing law be approved by at least 60% of voters, instead of a simple majority?"

Ballot text as printed in the Davis County 2026 General Election Notice. Note the ballot says "increase taxes"; the constitutional text says "includes."

Part D note: under Truth in Taxation the certified property-tax rate falls as values rise, so Part D reaches initiatives that keep revenue higher than the falling rate would, not ordinary tax cuts.

WORKED EXAMPLE · ROUND NUMBERS, NOT A REAL DISTRICT

A district collects $1,000,000 a year on $1 billion of taxable value. Its rate is 0.100%.

Next year, values rise 10%RateDistrict collects
Truth in Taxation (current law): rate falls so revenue stays the sameabout 0.091%$1,000,000
A citizen initiative holds the rate at 0.100% to fund schools0.100%$1,100,000

The initiative never raises the rate, but the rate "decreases less than current law would." Under Part D it needs 60%. A district can collect the same extra $100,000 today by holding a Truth in Taxation hearing, with no public vote.

OPEN QUESTION

Who decides whether an initiative "includes" a tax? S.J.R. 2 does not say, and no record reviewed for this page answers it. "Current law" in Part D is also undefined. Both questions go to the sponsors (see Questions to ask).

How and when you are told

  • Feb.–March 2025: The Legislature passes H.J.R. 10 (A) and S.J.R. 2 (B), about 20 months before the vote (Ballotpedia).
  • Today's rule: Proposed amendments must be published "in at least one newspaper in every county… for two months" before the election (Utah Const. Art. XXIII, Sec. 1).
  • If A passes: Notice runs "for 60 calendar days immediately preceding the next general election," by a method the Legislature sets by statute.
  • Voter information pamphlet: Arguments for and against each amendment are published by the Lt. Governor's office at vote.utah.gov.
  • Added Oct. 17, 2026: The 2026 Voter Information Pamphlet is posted. Amendment A: argument for by Rep. Anthony Loubet and Sen. Daniel McCay; no argument against was submitted, because the Legislature passed it unanimously. Amendment B: argument for by Sen. Lincoln Fillmore and Rep. Jason Kyle; argument against by Sen. Luz Escamilla and Rep. Angela Romero.
  • Oct. 13, 2026 (added Oct. 17): General-election ballots were scheduled to be mailed. The deadline to request a ballot is Oct. 27 at 5 p.m. (vote.utah.gov).
  • Nov. 3, 2026: General election.

How notice of A and B was actually published for 2026 is not documented on this page; it is listed under Questions to ask.

Added Oct. 17, 2026: The Salt Lake Tribune reported on Oct. 12, 2026 that both amendments on this year's ballot had to be published in newspapers, at an estimated cost of $1.4 million. The newspaper notices themselves were not found in news searches or Utah Public Notice Website search results on Oct. 17, so how and where they ran is still not documented here. In black and white

YES on A

  • Newspaper notice is no longer required by the Constitution.
  • The Legislature sets the notice method by statute.
  • Notice runs the 60 days before the election.

NO on A

  • The Constitution keeps requiring newspaper notice in every county for two months.
  • Changing the method later still takes a constitutional amendment.

YES on B

  • A citizen initiative that includes a tax increase, as S.J.R. 2 defines it, needs 60%: a new tax, a tax on more items, a higher rate, or a property-tax rate that falls less than current law would. Plain tax cuts still need a simple majority.
  • Initiatives with no tax still need a simple majority.
  • Lawmakers, counties, cities and districts are unchanged.

NO on B

  • Every statewide citizen initiative keeps the simple-majority rule.
  • The existing checks stay: legal review, cost estimate, 7 hearings, ~140,000 signatures from 26 of 29 Senate districts.
  • The Legislature can still amend initiatives afterward. Since July 2024, a change that impairs an initiative that reforms government must be narrowly tailored to a compelling government interest (Utah Supreme Court).

Sources: le.utah.gov, 2026 proposed constitutional amendments; S.J.R. 2 (2025); vote.utah.gov, "Instructions for a Statewide Initiative"; League of Women Voters v. Utah Legislature, Utah Supreme Court, July 11, 2024.

Corrected Oct. 7, 2026, after a reader comment: the YES column said "tax change," which could suggest tax cuts need 60%; it now says "tax increase, as S.J.R. 2 defines it." The NO column said the Legislature can amend "any initiative"; it now notes the 2024 limit on changes that impair government-reform initiatives.

Who Amendment B could help, and who it could hurt

Effects of how the rule works, not anyone's motives. Only the two named groups have taken a public position.

Could help

  • Anyone opposing a new tax: 40% + 1 now blocks a citizen tax initiative
  • Businesses with tax breaks or exemptions (closing a break = 60%), e.g. data centers at 0.5% vs. 6%
  • The Legislature: keeps more control over tax policy
  • Well-funded campaigns against an initiative
  • On record, supports: Utah Taxpayers Association; Taxpayer Shield PAC, chaired by S.J.R. 2 sponsor Sen. Lincoln Fillmore (Axios, Oct. 7, 2026; state PAC filing)

Could hurt

  • Initiatives that fund a program with a tax: health care (Prop 3), schools, water and the Great Salt Lake, parks, transit, housing
  • People those programs serve
  • "Fair share" and loophole-closing measures, even when most residents' taxes go down
  • Small, volunteer initiative campaigns
  • On record, opposes: Utahns Protecting Our Constitution

Not affected

  • The Legislature, county commissions, city councils, school boards, PID, MIDA and UIPA boards
  • Initiatives with no tax, and initiatives that only cut a tax

Sources: S.J.R. 2 (2025); KSL, Jan. 30, 2025 (Utah Taxpayers Association); state campaign filings (Utahns Protecting Our Constitution); MIDA–Box Elder interlocal §6(a); Ballotpedia (Prop 3).

Tap a year for the record

2018: Voters pass Props 2, 3 and 4

Prop 2 (medical cannabis) and Prop 3 (Medicaid, 0.15% sales tax) each passed with 53%. Prop 4 created an independent redistricting commission. (NORML; Ballotpedia; KJZZ)

Dec. 2018 – 2020: Lawmakers change all three

H.B. 3001 replaced Prop 2 and removed home growing (Dec. 3, 2018). S.B. 96 changed Prop 3 (signed Feb. 11, 2019). S.B. 200 replaced Prop 4 (2020).

Dec. 2019 – Jan. 2020: A citizen referendum stops a tax increase

S.B. 2001 raised the sales tax on food. Citizens filed 152,000 referendum signatures; lawmakers repealed it Jan. 27, 2020. (Ballotpedia)

July 11, 2024: Supreme Court rules on Prop 4

Unanimous: reform initiatives are protected from unlimited repeal. Justice Paige Petersen: "When Utahns exercise their right to reform the government through a citizen initiative, their exercise of these rights is protected from government infringement." (KJZZ)

Sept.–Oct. 2024: Amendments D and A voided

Newspaper notice was not published as the Constitution requires. Amendment D's ballot language was also found misleading. (FOX 13; KUER)

Feb.–March 2025: A and B referred to the 2026 ballot

H.J.R. 10 passed 70–0 and 29–0. S.J.R. 2 passed 21–8 and 55–17. (Ballotpedia)

Aug. 2025: Prop 4 repeal ruled unconstitutional

A district court struck the 2021 congressional map. (Ballotpedia, Aug. 29, 2025)

Nov. 3, 2026: Amendments A and B on your ballot

Both need a simple majority of voters to pass.

What voters passed vs. what the Legislature changed

Each 2018 initiative: what the law looked like before, what voters approved, and what happened after.

Prop 2: medical cannabis

Passed with 53% · No new tax

Before
2014 law let epilepsy patients possess certain CBD products, but "provided no legal in-state supply source" (NORML). In 2018, H.B. 195 allowed cannabis products for patients with "six months or less to live"; H.B. 197 (state growing) first failed 36–34 because it "needed 38 votes" (KSL), then passed later (NORML).
Voters passed
Home growing (6 plants) for patients over 100 miles from a dispensary; at least one dispensary per county; edibles; autoimmune disorders and "chronic or debilitating pain" as qualifying conditions. Fees were to cover about $1.4 million of ongoing costs; medical cannabis was exempt from sales tax (FOX 13, citing the Governor's budget office).
Legislature changed (H.B. 3001, Dec. 3, 2018)
No home growing; 5 licensed "pharmacies" with a pharmacist, plus a state central fill pharmacy; no edibles; flower only in blister packs; autoimmune disorders dropped and pain must last over two weeks without responding to conventional medicine; cities may zone pharmacies out of some areas (KSL; NORML).
Today
"Medical cannabis products are exempt from taxes, including the state sales tax" (Marijuana Policy Project).

Prop 3: Medicaid expansion

Passed with about 53% · 0.15% statewide sales tax

Before
2015: the House voted down Gov. Herbert's "Healthy Utah" plan. 2016: H.B. 437 covered "a little more than sixteen thousand Utahns" in extreme poverty (KUER, March 8, 2016).
Voters passed
Coverage up to 138% of the poverty level; federal government pays 90%, Utah 10%; a 0.15% sales tax for Utah's share.
Legislature changed (S.B. 96, Feb. 11, 2019)
Coverage cut to 100% of the poverty level from April 1, 2019; Utah's share under that plan 30%, not 10% (Jurist); the full 0.15% tax kept (Ballotpedia). Fiscal analysts estimated a "two-year cost of $49 million," about five times Prop 3's projected $10.4 million shortfall (Salt Lake Tribune, Feb. 4, 2019), while giving up $103 million (FY2019) and $407 million (FY2020) in federal funds (S.B. 96 fiscal note).
After
The federal government rejected the cap and would not pay 90% below 138% (Deseret News, Aug. 22, 2019). Full expansion began January 2020 with a work requirement, suspended April 2020 and later removed (healthinsurance.org). H.B. 15 (2026) ends expansion sooner if the federal share drops below 90% (le.utah.gov).

Prop 4: independent redistricting

Passed with 50.34% · No tax

Voters passed
An independent commission to draw maps and submit them to the Legislature; a ban on partisan gerrymandering.
Legislature changed (S.B. 200, 2020)
The commission became advisory, so the Legislature can ignore its maps; the partisan-gerrymandering ban was repealed (League of Women Voters).
After
July 2024: Utah Supreme Court protects government-reform initiatives from unlimited changes. Aug. 2025: district court rules the repeal unconstitutional and strikes the 2021 map (Ballotpedia). 2026: a Utah GOP-led initiative to repeal Prop 4 failed to qualify after signature withdrawals in Senate District 15 (Ballotpedia, March 28, 2026).

If Amendment B had applied in 2018, only Prop 3 would have needed 60%. B applies to votes after Nov. 1, 2026 and does not reach back.

The court in 2026

  • Jan. 2026: S.B. 134 expands the Utah Supreme Court from 5 to 7 justices. Chief Justice Durrant told lawmakers the court had "essentially no backlog"; the Utah State Bar said the bills would "fundamentally remake the state's judicial system" (Salt Lake Tribune, Jan. 31, 2026).
  • April–May 2026: Gov. Cox, Speaker Schultz and President Adams announce an investigation of Justice Diana Hagen, author of the 2024 Amendment D opinion; she resigns in May while the Utah GOP campaigns against her retention (Election Law Blog). Reports differ on the Judicial Conduct Commission's role.
  • June 17, 2026: Jay Jorgensen (22–7) and Stephen Dent (25–4) confirmed to the new seats; neither had been a judge (KUER).
  • Aug. 2026: Chief Justice Durrant retires Aug. 31. Matthew Bell (district judge since 2017) and Michael Menssen (no judicial experience) nominated Aug. 28 (KUER). With Justice Nielsen (2025), Gov. Cox would have appointed 5 of 7.

The citizen side of the ledger

  • 2019: The Legislature passed S.B. 2001 (Dec. 12, 2019), raising the sales tax on food. Citizens turned in 152,000 referendum signatures, and the Legislature repealed the law on Jan. 27, 2020. (Ballotpedia)
  • Utah Const. Art. I, Sec. 2: "All political power is inherent in the people; and all free governments are founded on their authority for their equal protection and benefit, and they have the right to alter or reform their government as the public welfare may require." (Justia)
