Amendment B: Myth vs. Fact

Short version
  • From 2022 to 2026 the state income-tax rate went down every year, from 4.95% to 4.45%. In the same years, other taxes went up or new ones were allowed. Amendment B would not change any of them.
  • A citizen initiative already goes through legal review, a cost estimate, 7 public hearings and about 140,000 signatures in 26 of 29 Senate districts. B changes only the vote needed at the end, for tax initiatives.
  • After voters passed Props 2, 3 and 4 in 2018, the Legislature replaced or rewrote all three. In July 2024 the Utah Supreme Court limited changes to government-reform initiatives.
  • Local property-tax increases, Weber County's 0.20% sales tax, and PID, MIDA and UIPA board decisions involve no public vote. B does not change them.
  • Sen. Lincoln Fillmore is the primary sponsor of S.J.R. 2 and Rep. Jason Kyle the House sponsor. This part lists related bills, who is connected to the record, and questions to ask.

What the text allows: hypothetical examples

These are not pending proposals. Each applies the actual wording of S.J.R. 2 to a hypothetical initiative.

HYPOTHETICAL

Equalizing an energy tax

Citizens propose charging data center campuses the same 6% energy tax other users pay in a MIDA project area, instead of "at least 0.5%." That is "an increase in an existing tax rate" (item C): 60%. (MIDA–Box Elder interlocal agreement §6(a))

HYPOTHETICAL

A tax swap that saves money

An initiative drops a $500 million tax and adds a $400 million one, a $100 million net cut. It "includes" a new tax (item A): it appears to need 60%.

HYPOTHETICAL

Property-tax relief that isn't "enough"

Citizens propose cutting a property-tax rate by 1% when current law would have cut it 2%. That is a rate that decreases "less than it would under current law" (item D): 60%.

HYPOTHETICAL

Ending a special exemption

Citizens propose ending a sales-tax exemption that only one industry receives. That applies a tax to "additional items or transactions" (item B): 60%.

What supporters say

The primary sponsor, Sen. Lincoln Fillmore, argued that 50.1% of voters shouldn't be able to raise taxes on the other 49.9%, and that lawmakers answer to voters at elections. He and Rep. Jason Kyle said B "leaves the decision with Utah voters while requiring broader agreement." The Utah Taxpayers Association supports it. (Utah News Dispatch, Sept. 28, 2026)

What opponents say

Utahns Protecting Our Constitution calls B an unnecessary, permanent change that weakens a tool citizens use to hold government accountable, noting the Legislature keeps a simple majority. (Utah News Dispatch, Sept. 28, 2026)

Questions to ask, and whom to ask

  • Sen. Lincoln Fillmore and Rep. Jason Kyle (sponsors)
    Does a tax swap that lowers total taxes need 60% under "includes"? When is "current law" measured?
  • Your state senator and representative
    Would you support the same 60% bar for tax increases passed by the Legislature? Would you sponsor that amendment?
  • Lt. Governor's office (elections)
    Are any statewide tax initiatives currently filed or in signature gathering?
  • Your county commission or city council
    When did you last raise property taxes or a local option tax, and was there a public vote?
  • Both campaigns
    Who are your largest donors? (Check disclosures.utah.gov.)

The collective questions

Applied to Amendment B only, from the record above.

  • Power: Raises the vote needed for one kind of citizen lawmaking; the Legislature's and local bodies' thresholds don't change.
  • Transparency: The ballot question says "increase taxes compared to existing law"; the text's trigger is "includes," and "current law" is undefined.
  • Financial accountability: Adds protection against citizen tax initiatives; no added protection against taxes from the Legislature, local boards, PIDs, MIDA or UIPA.
  • Environmental impact: No direct effect; measures funded by a new tax, such as resource protection, would face the 60% bar.
  • Community impact: Rural voters gain weight against statewide tax initiatives; statewide majorities between 50% and 59.9% on covered measures would no longer prevail.

Words to know

Initiative

A law proposed by citizens, put on the ballot by signature petition, and adopted by voters.

Referendum

A citizen challenge to a law the Legislature or a local body already passed, put to voters by petition.

Constitutional amendment

A change to the Utah Constitution. In Utah, only the Legislature can propose one, with two-thirds of each house; voters then approve it by simple majority.

Simple majority

More than half of the votes cast on the question.

Truth in Taxation

Utah's process requiring notice and a public hearing before a taxing entity collects more property tax than its certified rate would generate. No public vote is required.

PID

Public Infrastructure District: a special district that can levy assessments and issue bonds for infrastructure inside its boundary.
