The Checkbox: Who Decides What Voters See

A 2025 state law lets officials whose household includes an at-risk government employee have their employer removed from the public copy of their conflict-of-interest form. Here is the law, how it reached Weber County, and what a records appeals board decided on Oct. 13, 2026.

The short version

  • A 2025 state law, H.B. 504, lets an official or candidate ask that their employer information be removed from the public copy of their conflict-of-interest form if they, or their spouse, are an “at-risk government employee.” It took effect May 7, 2025.
  • When the request is made, the filing officer “shall redact.” Weber County's records appeals board called that “mandatory language triggered by receipt of a redaction request.” The board's decision does not describe any check of whether the official qualifies. At the Oct. 12, 2026 hearing, the county said it does not check whether an official qualifies, because the statute does not require it. The box was checked, and the county redacted.
  • On Oct. 12, 2026, the board heard the Hive publisher's appeal over Commissioner Gage Froerer's forms. Its Oct. 13 decision kept his 2026 employer lines redacted and ordered his 2025 lines released, because the 2025 form was filed before the law existed.
  • Of eight 2026 Weber County forms the Hive compared, three had employer lines redacted: Commissioner Froerer, Commissioner Harvey and County Attorney Allred. County attorneys are a named category in the law. County commissioners are not.
  • Added Oct. 18, 2026: Froerer's forms have changed over the years. His 2018 House form listed Nordic Valley Land Associates without a percentage; a November 2022 special disclosure, filed after an Ogden Valley resident complained to the county attorney and state auditor, showed a family trust's 16% interest; his 2025 county form listed the company and his 2026 form, filed a week before it was dissolved, did not. His Weber Housing Authority trusteeship was not listed on either county form.
  • Added Oct. 18, 2026: Under state law the $100 fine and class B misdemeanor apply when an official does not file, or does not correct a form within seven days after notice. Whether a form is accurate is examined only after a written complaint. In the one review the Hive has documented, in 2022, the result was an updated form and no public penalty.
  • The law still points to Section 63G-2-303 for the at-risk definition. The board noted that section was renumbered in 2026 and “no longer exists.”

What this page does and does not say
This page reports what the law says, what the county did and what the appeals board decided. It does not say any official acted improperly. The law allows the request, and the board found the county was required to honor it.

What the checkbox is

Elected officials file a conflict-of-interest form so voters can see who pays them and what businesses they are tied to. Since May 7, 2025, part of that form can be removed from the public copy.

Item 1 of the form asks for the official's current and recent employers and a short description of the job. Under the 2025 change, an official whose household includes an at-risk government employee can ask the filing officer to take that information out. This is the law, as quoted in the appeals board's decision:

“(7)(a) In making the disclosure described in Subsection (6)(b) or (h), if a regulated officeholder or regulated officeholder's spouse is an at-risk government employee, as that term is defined in Subsection 63G-2-303(1)(a), the regulated officeholder may request the filing officer to redact from the conflict of interest disclosure: (i) the regulated officeholder's employment information under Subsection (6)(b); and (ii) the regulated officeholder's spouse's name and employment information under Subsection (6)(h).

(b) A filing officer who receives a redaction request under Subsection (7)(a) shall redact the disclosures made under Subsection (6)(b) or (h) before the filing officer makes the conflict of interest disclosure available for public inspection.”

Utah Code 20A-11-1604(7), as quoted in the Weber County GRAMA Appeals Board Decision and Order, Oct. 13, 2026, page 2. Same text in the state's posted version effective May 7, 2025.

Who counts as “at-risk”

The definition lists named jobs, then adds a general category. The named jobs are peace officers; state and federal judges and court commissioners; military judges; federal and military prosecutors; and “law enforcement officials,” a term that includes county attorneys, district attorneys, the attorney general and city prosecutors (Utah Code 53-5a-311). Sheriffs are peace officers under Utah Code 53-13-103. The last item, the one quoted by the board, covers everyone else:

“(ix) state, federal, or local government employee who, because of the unique nature of the employee's regular work assignments or because of one or more recent credible threats directed to or against the employee, would be at immediate and substantial risk of physical harm if the employee's personal information is disclosed.”

Utah Code 63A-19-408(1)(a)(ix), formerly 63G-2-303(1)(a), as quoted in the Decision and Order, page 3; full list in the state's posted version effective May 6, 2026.
In plain words: a county commissioner is not one of the named jobs. A commissioner could still fit item (ix) if their work or a recent credible threat puts them at real risk of harm. The public copy of the form does not show which part of the definition an official relied on.

The bill's own summary says the request is open to candidates too. H.B. 504 permits “the candidate or officeholder making the disclosure to request the redaction of certain personally identifiable information from the disclosure if the candidate, officeholder, or the candidate's or officeholder's spouse is an at-risk government employee” (H.B. 504 enrolled copy, highlighted provisions). Because the test is about being a government employee, a candidate who does not work for a government would not qualify.

An elected official, a list he is not on, and a box no one checks

Added Oct. 18, 2026. This is the center of the case, step by step, from the law and the Oct. 13, 2026 decision.

  1. He holds a county job because voters elected him. His 2025 form lists his current employer as “Weber County Corp. 2017 to present” and his job as “Weber County Commissioner” (2025 form, Items 1A–1B). The at-risk definition's named categories are other kinds of jobs: peace officers; state and federal judges and court commissioners; military judges; federal and military prosecutors; and “law enforcement officials” as defined in Section 53-5a-311 (Utah Code 63A-19-408(1)(a)(i)–(viii), read Oct. 18, 2026).
  2. County commissioners are not a named category. The only route is the catch-all in item (ix), which the board quoted in finding 6: a “state, federal, or local government employee who, because of the unique nature of the employee's regular work assignments or because of one or more recent credible threats directed to or against the employee, would be at immediate and substantial risk of physical harm if the employee's personal information is disclosed.”
  3. Both routes depend on facts. Item (ix) needs either unusual work duties or recent credible threats. The 2026 form's box asked him to state that he was “an at-risk government employee . . . as that term is defined in Subsection 63G-2-303(1)(a)” (finding 7). It does not ask which route. At the Oct. 12, 2026 hearing, the county said it does not check whether an official qualifies, because the statute does not require it. The box was checked, and the county redacted.
  4. The checkmark decides it. The board found the filing officer had to redact because the law “uses mandatory language triggered by receipt of a redaction request” (finding 8); the law says the filing officer “shall redact.” Separately, the written decision describes no review step. The county, the board and the public do not see what the claim rests on.
  5. What is covered is more than the government job. His county job is already public. The redaction covers the full employer lines, including outside employers. On his 2025 form, those lines included “Rescom, Inc. and or Gage Froerer & Assoc. Inc. 1977-Present.”

How it got here

The checkbox came from H.B. 504 (2025), “Financial and Conflict of Interest Disclosures by Candidates Amendments.”

The primary sponsor was Rep. Lisa Shepherd. The Senate sponsor was Sen. Ronald M. Winterton (both as printed on the enrolled bill). LegiScan also lists Rep. Paul Cutler as a sponsor. Most of the bill was about something else: it requires county, city and special district candidates to file conflict-of-interest forms when they file for office, and it sets rules for filling midterm vacancies. Why the county said no

The county relied on “Utah Code Ann. § 20A-11-1604(7)(a) and Utah Code Ann. § 63A-19-408(1),” which it said “required redaction of information regarding current and previous employment and information regarding other individuals in Commissioner Froerer's household” (Decision, finding 2).

At the hearing, the county said the 2025 form was first posted unredacted, then redacted after the records request “to make them both consistent” with the 2026 form (The Weber County Hive, Oct. 13, 2026).

What the board decided

The board met in closed session twice, for about 40 minutes and then about 15 minutes, and voted 3–0 (The Weber County Hive, Oct. 13, 2026). Its written decision, signed by Chair Tanya Strong and dated Oct. 13, 2026, split the request in two.

Why the 2025 form was redacted too

Added Oct. 18, 2026. The county redacted the 2025 form because of the 2026 checkbox, so the two would match. In order:

  1. The 2025 form, dated Jan. 24, 2025, had no at-risk box (finding 10) and was first posted unredacted.
  2. After the 2026 box was checked and the records request came in, the county redacted the 2025 form too, “to make them both consistent,” as the county said at the Oct. 12 hearing (The Weber County Hive, Oct. 13, 2026).
  3. The board found “there was no legal basis for the County to apply new § 20A-11-1604(7) retroactively, so it should not have redacted portions of Commissioner Froerer's 2025 conflict of interest disclosure” (finding 12), and ordered it released.

Appeal rights

What happens when a form is incomplete

Added Oct. 18, 2026. The rules as written, then what has happened in practice. This describes the rules and outcomes, not anyone's motives.

The rules as written

  • The penalty is for not filing or not responding. The top of Froerer's 2025 county form says: “An elected officer who fails to respond to the notice is guilty of a class B misdemeanor and the county clerk must impose a civil fine of $100.” (2025 form, page 1.)
  • The state law sets the same steps. Utah Code 20A-11-1605 (posted version effective May 7, 2025, read Oct. 18, 2026 through a text copy) calls for a review “Within 60 calendar days” after the filing deadline; notice to an officeholder who did not file or whose form does not meet Section 1604, with a direction to file an amended report; and, for anyone who does not file or amend “within seven calendar days after the day on which the regulated officeholder receives the notice,” a class B misdemeanor and “a civil fine of $100.” The section names the lieutenant governor as the reviewing officer; the Hive's Housing Authority case describes the same steps for county forms.
  • Accuracy is examined after a written complaint. Under the same section, a “written complaint alleging a violation of Section 20A-11-1604” leads to “notice and an opportunity to be heard” before a decision. In the text read, no separate penalty is listed for a form found to be inaccurate; the listed penalties apply to not filing or not amending within seven days. (Checked from a text copy of the posted section, not page images.)
  • The checkbox has no review step. The filing officer “shall redact” once the request is made (finding 8). At the Oct. 12, 2026 hearing, the county said it does not check whether an official qualifies, because the statute does not require it.
  • Disclosure, not recusal. The county attorney's office told reporters the law required disclosure, not recusal (Standard-Examiner, 2023; UPR, 2023). The county's ethics act itself (Utah Code 17-16a) would not load on Oct. 18, 2026, so this page relies on those published statements.
  • The county act's stronger penalty. Weber County's acknowledgment of the County Officers and Employees Disclosure Act says knowingly and intentionally violating it is a class A misdemeanor and requires removal from office (Housing Authority case).

What has happened in practice

The Hive's Enforcement Gap case found the same path in three Utah cases: question → complaint → review → updated or defended report → no public penalty. For Froerer in 2022, it reported: “Froerer filed an updated disclosure in November 2022. The Weber County Attorney's Office subsequently said the updated disclosure satisfied state law.”

  • Commissioner Harvey's 2025 form, dated Jan. 2, 2025, showed “Weber County” and “Weber County Commissioner – seat C” with nothing removed. That form, like Froerer's, was filed before the law took effect.
Fair reading
Checking the box is allowed by law. Not checking it is also allowed. Nothing on this page says any official acted improperly; it shows where the box was used and what the public copy no longer shows.

The same information elsewhere

Froerer also sits as a trustee of the Weber Basin Water Conservancy District, which posts its own trustee conflict-of-interest forms.

His “Annual Trustee Conflict of Interest Disclosure,” completed Jan. 12, 2026, lists his employer as “Weber County Corp” with the job “County Commissioner,” and lists real estate entities described as “Real estate brokerage and management.” Nothing on it is redacted, and it has no at-risk box (Weber Basin Water Conservancy District, posted form).

At the hearing, the county said the water district “probably did not get a form” claiming at-risk status (The Weber County Hive, Oct. 13, 2026).

Why it matters: the same employer information for the same official, from the same month, is redacted on one government's website and posted in full on another's. The redaction law applies to the filing officer who receives the request.

How these forms are checked

The only person who fills out a conflict-of-interest form is the official it describes. Here is what happens to the at-risk box after the form is turned in.

The path of a checked box

From Utah Code 20A-11-1604(7), the Oct. 13, 2026 Decision and Order, and Utah Code 20A-11-1605.

flowchart TD A["The official fills out
and signs the form"] --> B["The official checks
the at-risk box"] B --> C["Filing officer
#quot;shall redact#quot;"] C --> D["The public sees
blacked-out employer lines"] D --> E["Only path to review:
a records request
and an appeal"]
  • The official signs; no one else confirms. The form asks the official to state that it is “true and accurate to the best of” their knowledge (Utah Code 20A-11-1604; the same words appear on Froerer's 2025 form).
  • The review checks that forms were filed, not whether they are right. The filing officer reviews whether each required person filed and whether the form has the required information (Utah Code 20A-11-1605).
  • The box triggers a duty, not a review. The appeals board found the filing officer had to redact because the law “uses mandatory language triggered by receipt of a redaction request” (Decision, finding 8). The decision does not describe a step where anyone checks eligibility, and at the Oct. 12, 2026 hearing the county said it does not check whether an official qualifies, because the statute does not require it.
  • Accuracy is examined only after a complaint. A written complaint leads to notice and a hearing before the filing officer decides whether there was a violation (Utah Code 20A-11-1605). The Hive's Weber Housing Authority case walks through this in more detail.

The out-of-date cross-reference

The checkbox law borrows its definition of “at-risk government employee” from another part of the code. That other part moved.

Where the definition points

From the Decision and Order, finding 3, and H.B. 450 (2026).

flowchart TD A["Checkbox law
20A-11-1604(7)(a)"] -->|"still points to"| B["Section 63G-2-303
#quot;no longer exists#quot;"] B -->|"renumbered May 6, 2026"| C["Section 63A-19-408
same definition"]

At the Oct. 12, 2026 hearing, the appellant told the board she could not find the section the law cites for the at-risk definition, Section 63G-2-303. The board's written decision explains why. The board wrote: “Section 63A-19-408 was renumbered from 63G-2-303 as of May 6, 2026, but it appears that the Legislature has not yet updated § 20A-11-1604(7)(a) to reflect that numbering change; § 20A-11-1604(7)(a) still refers to § 63G-2-303, which no longer exists. The applicable portions of § 63A-19-408 have remained the same at all times relevant to this appeal” (Decision, finding 3).

Froerer's 2026 form uses the old reference too. The board's finding 7 says the form's box stated he was “an at-risk government employee . . . as that term is defined in Subsection 63G-2-303(1)(a).”

Because the wording of the definition stayed the same, the board quoted and applied the definition as it now appears in Section 63A-19-408(1) (“formerly found in § 63G-2-303(1)”, finding 6). The out-of-date number did not change the outcome: the board upheld the 2026 redaction.

In plain words: it is like a sign that gives an old street address. The building moved, the people inside did not change, and the board read the law as meaning the new address.

Where the move came from. The renumbering was done by H.B. 450 (2026), “Data Privacy Amendments” — primary sponsor Rep. David Shallenberger, Senate sponsor Sen. Kirk A. Cullimore, signed March 19, 2026 (LegiScan). One earlier draft, the 5th Substitute, also updated the checkbox law to point to the new section. The enrolled (final) copy's list of amended sections, read Oct. 17, 2026, does not include 20A-11-1604. The record read for this page does not say why that change was dropped.

What this means for voters

  • The form is still filed. Every official in the comparison filed a 2026 form. What changes with the box is how much of Item 1 the public copy shows.
  • The rule is the same for everyone who qualifies. The law applies to officials and candidates whose household includes an at-risk government employee, in every county and city covered.
  • Some information can still be found. Other filings, such as business entity listings on the same form or forms for other boards, may still show it. The Weber Basin trustee form is one example.
  • Old forms are covered by old rules. The board found the law cannot be applied to forms filed before May 7, 2025.
  • Changes to the law are up to the Legislature. The appeals board said it was bound by the statute as written.
