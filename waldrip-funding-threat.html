Wrong Room, Right Message

The governor's senior housing adviser came to Heber City to talk about state housing policy. Before he left, he threatened to cut off Wasatch County's funding — and said, on the record, that the room he was in wasn't who the message was for.

SB 258 / preliminary municipality
A 2024 Utah law letting up to three landowners petition the state directly to create a brand-new town on their land — without needing approval from the county the land currently sits in.
B&C road funds
"Class B and C" money: a fixed share of state gas-tax revenue that Utah law requires be divided among counties and cities every quarter, using a set formula based on population and road mileage — not a discretionary grant.

Steve Waldrip came to the Heber City Council on August 18 to talk about the state's housing plan. He left having threatened to zero out state funding to Wasatch County — a message he delivered not to the county itself, but to the city next door.

The Setup

+

Waldrip, the governor's senior adviser for housing strategy and innovation, was the invited speaker for the third session of Heber City's Affordable Housing 101 series, a public education program the city started in May 2026. His assigned topic, per the city's own staff report: "State Housing Plans & Legislation … What is the state's plan for housing?" Co-presenting was Tony Kohler, Heber City's Community Development Director.

Added Oct. 17, 2026: the official amended agenda on the Utah Public Notice Website lists the item as "5 pm: Affordable Housing 101: State Housing Plans & Legislation. What is the state's plan for housing? (Steve Waldrip, Governor's Office) (Tony Kohler, Community Development Director) - 60 min." The staff report in the packet itself did not open for this check. Separately, the Wasatch County Housing Authority Board's July 20, 2026 minutes note that "Steve Waldrip from the Governor's office will be up to discuss the new State initiatives and programs." Links are in Sources.

Nothing in the official agenda or staff report previewed what came next.

The Threat

+

Partway through the meeting, addressing Heber City Mayor Heidi Franco, Waldrip delivered a message on behalf of Gov. Spencer Cox.

"So my message back from our good governor, who cares deeply about these issues is: Your funding as a county is now at risk, meaning none. Anything within my power, I will stop."

Steve Waldrip · Heber City Council, Aug. 18, 2026

He went further: "Without being thoughtful, without having discussions, with just saying we couldn't care less … That funding is now suspended." Waldrip said Summit and Wasatch counties had "declared war" on the state over growth issues.

The trigger, according to Waldrip: Wasatch County's decision not to move forward with a legislative development agreement proposed by Philo Development — 144 lots on 740 acres of School and Institutional Trust Lands (SITLA), in an area currently zoned for one unit per 160 acres — and the county's decision to join Summit County in calling for the repeal of Senate Bill 258.

Wrong Room

+
Not the Named Party

There was one problem: Waldrip wasn't talking to Wasatch County. He was talking to Heber City — a separate municipal government with no authority over the county's decisions on SITLA or SB 258.

Wasatch County Manager Dustin Grabau, responding afterward, said the county hadn't broken any law — only advocated for a policy change. He said the county has reached out to the Governor's Office and hopes to meet the week of August 24, calling the exchange so far "a one-sided conversation." He said he wasn't aware of "any public discourse that conveys the county is defying state law," and called the public threat "a little confusing … a little bewildering," with "no basis in reality."

Added Oct. 17, 2026, quote check: the Park Record's Aug. 21 article gives Grabau's words as "without any basis in reality." KPCW's Aug. 21 report quotes him saying, "So, it's a little bewildering." Links are in Sources.

Heber City Councilmember Yvonne Barney pushed back at the same meeting. She said the friction over the SITLA land was really about the specific developer — one that had drawn similar pushback in other communities — not defiance of the state. She said the issue "could have been conflated by media attention or misunderstanding," and added: "if our governor is trying to use the tactics of threats to our community, that doesn't go over well, either."

Waldrip also called the counties' opposition to SB 258 "selfish," saying public comments on the issue tend to focus on personal impact: "People have forgotten that they impacted somebody at one point in time, and it was OK." He said the conversation needs to happen "differently" and invited county leaders to contact him directly.

The Money He Named Is Not Discretionary

+

Class B and C road funds aren't an ordinary grant program the Governor's office can turn on or off at will. Under Utah Code, 30% of qualifying state highway-user tax revenue is appropriated annually for B&C roads, and the statute says the money "shall be apportioned" among counties and municipalities. Each county's share is set by a fixed formula — half based on population, half on weighted road mileage — and the Utah Department of Transportation is required to transfer the funds into local accounts at least quarterly.

State administrative rules do allow UDOT — not the Governor's office — to withhold future allocations, but only for specific compliance failures tied to how the money is used: unauthorized spending, missed audits, inaccurate road mileage reporting, or improperly closing a qualifying road to public travel, among others. Nothing in the statute or the rule identifies opposition to a development agreement, criticism of state housing policy, or advocacy for a law's repeal as grounds for withholding a county's allocation. Neither Cox's office nor Waldrip publicly identified a compliance violation when he told the Heber City Council that Wasatch County's funding was "at risk, meaning none" and had been "suspended."

That leaves an open question the statute itself raises: was Waldrip describing an actual UDOT compliance action against Wasatch County — or asserting a new, informal condition on money the Legislature directed the state to distribute by formula? Nothing reviewed for this story shows the county has been found out of compliance, notified of a violation, or had a quarterly distribution actually withheld.

Added Oct. 17, 2026: KPCW's Aug. 21 report says Waldrip cited "B and C road funds" among the state funding at risk. Utah News Dispatch reported on Sept. 21 that Wasatch County receives "roughly $2.1 million a year in state B and C road funding." The current text of state rule R926-3-3(1)(e), as posted by Cornell's Legal Information Institute, says UDOT "has the authority to withhold future allocations" if a local highway authority "fails to comply with applicable law regarding the use of class B or class C road funds." Rule R926-3-7 says eligible roads "shall always remain open to public travel."

Added Oct. 17, 2026, statute check: the Legislature's current posted text of Title 72, Chapter 2, Part 1 (Section 72-2-107 shows "Amended by Chapter 344, 2026 General Session") says the department receives "an amount equal to 30% of" a computed amount of highway-user revenue for class B and C roads, and Section 72-2-107(3) begins, "At least quarterly the department shall make the necessary accounting entries" to transfer it. Section 72-2-108(2)(a) says the funds "shall be apportioned among counties and municipalities," 50% by weighted road mileage and 50% by population. This matches the description above.

The Same Night, A Different Fight

+

The Aug. 18 meeting wasn't only about Waldrip. Later that evening, the council took up two resolutions tied to Heber City's own budget fight: Resolution 2026-18, adopting a certified tax rate for fiscal year 2027, and Resolution 2026-20 — titled, in the city's own agenda language, "Adopting Fiscal Year Final 2027 Budget Including Heber City CRA."

That wording matters. At the city's Truth in Taxation public hearing two weeks earlier, on August 5, multiple residents connected the city's budget shortfall directly to its Community Reinvestment Area (CRA) — the mechanism that diverts a portion of property tax growth away from the general fund and into a designated development zone. One speaker, a Midway City Council member, argued the CRA was capturing normal market-value growth that had nothing to do with blight, the condition CRAs are legally meant to address, and cited a valuation error that briefly would have let the CRA capture all of the property's taxable value before it was caught. He asked the council to return the diverted funds, cut the tax rate, and terminate the CRA.

City Manager Matt Brower disputed the connection at that hearing, telling residents the CRA was not the reason for the Truth in Taxation process. But the city's own Aug. 18 agenda item — bundling the CRA explicitly into the same budget resolution — cuts against that separation.

Added Oct. 17, 2026: the city's draft Aug. 18 minutes record both resolutions passing 5–0. The council adopted a 4.2% increase instead of the proposed 4.8%, and the CRA fund amount was given as $370,000. On Sept. 14, KPCW reported the council expected to roll back the 4.2% increase: City Manager Matt Brower said a State Tax Commission review found the city had not posted the required combined tax statement on its website for 14 days before the hearing, so the increase could not be imposed. The Aug. 5 hearing was noticed and held, and approved minutes for it are posted on the state notice site.

Analysis — a familiar shape

There's a pattern worth naming here, separate from the question of who Waldrip's message was actually meant for. A Community Reinvestment Area works by quietly redirecting property tax growth away from a city's general fund and into a separate account, justified under a "blight" designation that residents at Heber City's own Truth in Taxation hearing argued doesn't fit a healthy downtown. The money is still moving — it's just moving somewhere other than where the average taxpayer assumes it's going, under a label that obscures the mechanism.

Waldrip's threat worked the same way, structurally. A message meant for Wasatch and Summit counties was delivered instead to Heber City, dressed up as a routine housing-policy presentation, with Waldrip himself later acknowledging the city wasn't the real target — it was simply the room where the message would eventually find its way to the people it was meant for. Different money, different actors, same basic move: obscure where the pressure, or the diversion, is actually coming from.

A Pattern, Not an Isolated Incident

+

Wasatch County isn't the only county to describe pressure from the state over a growth-related decision this year. In Box Elder County, commissioners voted in June to approve zoning tied to the Stratos data center project after, according to Commissioner Lee Perry, the Utah Inland Port Authority (MIDA) told them they could support the process — or the project would continue without them, "and potentially lead to lawsuits from the developer."

Correction note added Oct. 17, 2026: MIDA is Utah's Military Installation Development Authority (Utah Public Radio, June 24, 2026). It is a separate state body from the Utah Inland Port Authority. Source found Oct. 17, 2026: the Deseret News (Brigham Tomco, June 5, 2026) reported that Perry said MIDA told commissioners the project "would continue without them, and potentially lead to lawsuits from the developer." The story names MIDA, not the Inland Port Authority. Link in Sources.

That is a different mechanism than what Waldrip described to Heber City — a legal-bypass and litigation threat, not a funding cutoff — and it should not be conflated with the Wasatch County situation. But together, the two cases describe a broader pattern: county officials in more than one part of the state say they've faced state-level pressure, delivered outside normal channels, tied to development decisions the state wanted to go a different way.
