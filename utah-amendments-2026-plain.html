What is on the ballot?

Two changes to the Utah Constitution. The Legislature wrote both and passed both in 2025. Now voters decide. Each one passes if more than half of voters say yes.

This page explains what they do. It does not tell you how to vote.

Amendment A: how you are told

Today the Constitution says a proposed amendment must be printed in a newspaper in every county for two months before the vote.

Amendment A takes the newspaper rule out. Lawmakers would pick the method by ordinary law, for the 60 days before the election. A law already passed for this, H.B. 481, says the notice would go on the state's public notice website, the Lieutenant Governor's website, and a posting in a public place.

Why now? In 2024, courts threw out two amendments because the newspaper notice was not printed. Lawmakers passed Amendment A 70–0 and 29–0. The state voter pamphlet says it could save up to $1.4 million every two years. More in Part 1.

Amendment B: 60% for some citizen laws

Citizens can write a law and put it on the ballot. That is called an initiative. Today it passes with more than half of the votes.

Amendment B says an initiative needs 60% if it includes a new tax, a tax on more things, a higher tax rate, or a property-tax rate that falls less than it would under current law. Initiatives with no tax, or that only cut a tax, still need just over half.

What vote does it take?

Share of the vote needed to approve.

Citizen tax initiative, today
50% + 1
Citizen tax initiative, if B passes
60%
Legislature passing a tax
50% + 1
Legislature putting an amendment on the ballot
two-thirds

B does not change the Legislature, cities, counties or districts. Lawmakers still pass taxes with a majority of each house.

An example: in 2018 voters passed Prop 3, Medicaid expansion with a small sales tax, with 53%. Under B it would have needed 60%. B only covers votes after Nov. 1, 2026. It does not undo anything.

One question is still open: the amendment does not say who decides whether an initiative "includes" a tax. More in Part 2.

What people are saying

We checked 44 public comments and 20 common claims against the record. Some were right, some were partly right, and some were not supported.

We also found a 2026 proposal that went the other way. H.J.R. 20 would have made voters approve most state and local tax increases. It never got a hearing. More in Part 3.

What voters don't decide at all

Many tax and debt choices get no public vote. Examples: $5.267 billion in bonds from special districts called PIDs, and Weber County's new 0.20% sales tax. Neither amendment changes those. Part 4 lists the tax tools lawmakers created, who is connected, questions to ask, and words to know. More in Part 4.

What's new

Ballots went out starting Oct. 13. Election Day is Nov. 3. One poll in September found about half of voters for B, about one in five against, and many unsure. Groups on both sides are running ads.
