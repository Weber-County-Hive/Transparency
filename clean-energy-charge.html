The Clean Energy Charge on Your Power Bill

What the Community Clean Energy Program is, who pays, where the money goes, and how the opt-in version of the law was dropped — explained from the ground up.

The short version. If you get a Rocky Mountain Power bill at a home or business in one of 16 Utah communities, a new charge starts January 1, 2027 — unless you opt out. Homes pay a flat $4 a month. Businesses pay by how much power they use. You can leave free until May 1, 2027. After that, homes pay $30 to leave. A version of the 2026 bill that would have made this sign-up-only (opt-in) was drafted, but the version that passed kept automatic enrollment.

1. Start here: the basics

In plain words Rocky Mountain Power is a private company — it is owned by PacifiCorp, which is owned by Berkshire Hathaway. It is the only company allowed to sell electricity to most homes in northern and central Utah, so customers cannot shop around. Because there is no competition, a state board decides what it may charge.

Opt-in vs. opt-out

In plain words Opt-in means you are not in unless you sign up. Opt-out means you are in unless you say no. This program is opt-out: if you do nothing, you are enrolled and you pay.

Why is there a fee to leave?

In plain words The fee is written into state law. The 2019 law (Utah Code 54-17-905) says that after the free window closes, a customer who leaves must give notice and pay a termination charge set by the PSC. The law says the charge may include the cost of power projects bought for the program that are no longer being used by participants, so those costs do not shift to other customers. In other words: the program signs long-term contracts based on how many people are in it, and the fee is meant to cover the share of those contracts a departing customer leaves behind.
  • The law requires a free window of at least three billing cycles. The PSC extended it to six months for current customers (through May 1, 2027) at the communities' request.
  • No fee for: low-income Schedule 3 homes; customers who move out of a participating community; customers who close their account; anyone enrolled by mistake (who also get a refund of up to one year).
  • An unpaid fee cannot be used to shut off a home's power (tariff special condition 3).
  • The PSC can change the fee amount.

Who set the $30 — and where the fee money goes

In plain words The leaving fees were set by the communities' agency, not by Rocky Mountain Power. The agency's board adopted them unanimously on Oct. 7, 2024. When a board member asked whether any technical or financial analysis backed the numbers, the minutes record no analysis — only that the committee wanted the home fee to be affordable. When state regulators asked Rocky Mountain Power how the fees were figured, the company said the agency developed them and that it does not have the justification for the amounts. No record found says which fund the fee money goes into.

Why doesn't the power company pay for it?

In plain words Two reasons appear in the records. First, a regulated power company does not pay for power plants out of its own pocket in the end — customers pay through rates for whatever the PSC approves. Second, this program buys clean power beyond what Rocky Mountain Power plans to buy for all customers, because these 16 communities asked for more. The 2019 law says the extra cost must be paid by the customers in those communities and must not shift to anyone else. So the law itself puts the cost on participants, not on the company or its shareholders.
  • Rocky Mountain Power's testimony says participants pay all of the extra resource and administrative costs.
  • The projects are owned by private developers and bought under contracts, not built by Rocky Mountain Power. That is different from a company-owned plant, where the company can earn an approved return.
  • Rocky Mountain Power's own costs to run the program — including a full-time administrator — are charged to the program, not absorbed by the company.

Open No record read shows Rocky Mountain Power contributing any of its own money, or earning a profit on the program. Neither is confirmed either way.

Can the amount change?

In plain words Yes. The PSC can change the rates and the leaving fee. Rocky Mountain Power files a rate update every year. Its own projections show the rate dropping after the first few years and going below zero around 2043–2049, when savings would be returned to participants. Those are projections, not promises. The low-income surcharge cannot go above $0.70 a month.

6. Where the $4 goes

In plain words You do not get different electricity. Everyone gets the same power through the same wires. The money goes into a pot. Private companies build new solar, wind or geothermal plants, and Rocky Mountain Power agrees to buy their power. The pot backs those deals. Before a power contract can take effect, the pot must hold enough to cover about 12½ years of that plant's power.

Who owns the plants, and does Rocky Mountain Power profit?

In plain words Private developers build and own the plants. Rocky Mountain Power buys their power under long-term contracts, and the program's savings pot backs those contracts. Every record read so far describes the program as cost recovery — participants pay its costs. No record read says whether Rocky Mountain Power earns a profit on it, so that question is still open, not answered.

Open Profit or return to Rocky Mountain Power — not addressed either way in the PSC order or testimony read.

7. The opt-in version that was dropped (H.B. 238, 2026)

In plain words In February 2026 a draft of House Bill 238 would have changed the program to sign-up-only. That draft was replaced the next day by a version that kept automatic enrollment and added a simple opt-out check box. The final law is opt-out. No record found so far shows a vote on the opt-in draft or explains why it was dropped.

9. Who decided — and what the PSC said

The PSC order of March 4, 2026 (Docket 25-035-06) was signed by Chair Jerry D. Fenn, Commissioner David R. Clark and Commissioner John S. Harvey, Ph.D. No dissent appears in the order.

  • Rocky Mountain Power, the Division of Public Utilities and the Office of Consumer Services recommended waiting until a power contract was signed. The PSC approved the program effective immediately, citing expiring federal tax credits.
  • Rocky Mountain Power and the Office of Consumer Services opposed a flat fee for homes. The PSC accepted the communities' request for a flat $4.
  • The PSC wrote that it is generally not its role to protect residents from the decisions of their local elected leaders.
  • The PSC found the record did not support Rocky Mountain Power's method for valuing projects, and left that question for a later proceeding.

What the state's consumer advocates warned

In plain words Neither state office asked the PSC to reject the program, but both raised risks. The Office of Consumer Services said customers would be paying before anyone knew what the power would cost. The Division of Public Utilities warned that if many people opt out, the program could struggle to keep going.
OfficeConcern raised in testimony
Office of Consumer Services (Anthony Sandonato, Oct. 2025)Until a power contract is signed, the rate is speculative; recommended no notices or charges until then. Participants pay into reserves in advance, and a project could end early or never be built. Real costs could end up higher than estimated. Preferred charging by usage over a flat fee, saying a flat fee makes low-use homes pay more for what they get.

The federal tax credit deadline

At the Dec. 16, 2025 hearing, Rocky Mountain Power's witness agreed developers needed to start construction before July 4, 2026 to keep federal production tax credits. That date comes from the 2025 federal law (P.L. 119-21). Open Whether either chosen project actually started construction in time is not shown in records found.

Still being decided at the PSC

  • Docket 26-035-38 (reporting and how projects are valued): opened Aug. 25, 2026. The PSC ordered Rocky Mountain Power to file proposed reporting requirements by Dec. 31, 2026, with a comment period after.
  • Oct. 26, 2026 order: the PSC approved a change so customers who move no longer carry their enrollment to the new address.
  • Residential surcharge: a PSC clarification on June 12, 2026 set the low-income surcharge inside the $4 at $0.10 for homes. Ogden's May 5 minutes describe it as $3.88 + $0.12; the $0.12 figure now applies to businesses only.

Public comments to the PSC

The hearing transcript counts 41 public comments as of Dec. 16, 2025. Several comment files came through an online letter tool sent on behalf of people associated with the Sierra Club Utah Chapter, mostly supporting the program while objecting to parts of Rocky Mountain Power's plan. One commenter asked for a "will not exceed" rate so customers would know the most they could pay.

12. Words you will see

Tariff / Schedule
The official price sheet the PSC approves. This program is "Schedule 100."
Docket
A case file at the PSC, with a number like 25-035-06.
PPA (power purchase agreement)
A contract to buy power from a plant at a set price for many years.
Developer
The private company that builds and owns the power plant.
Reserve
The savings pot built from customers' monthly charges.
Net-100%
Over a year, enough clean power is added to the grid to equal what participants use. Your power still comes from the normal mix.
Unincorporated
Areas outside any city, run by the county.
Kilowatt-hour (kWh)
The unit your power use is measured in on your bill.
Production tax credit
A federal tax break for clean energy projects, with deadlines.
Termination fee
The fee to leave after the free window closes.
Substitute bill
A rewritten version of a bill that replaces the earlier version.
