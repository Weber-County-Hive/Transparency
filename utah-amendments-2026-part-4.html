Taxes and tax tools the Legislature created

Amendment B sets a 60% bar for citizen initiatives. These are the taxes and tax tools the Legislature itself created or expanded. Each one passed by a simple majority of each house, and in none of them do voters approve the final tax or zone.

The full count: 62 bills from 2015 to 2026 that changed state taxes or fees or gave taxing authority to local bodies, each with its status, fiscal note and sources, are in our Taxation repo: Utah's Tax Changes: 62 Bills in Our 2015–2026 Inventory.

Some of these tools come with checks. Some need a review committee or a participation agreement with the affected taxing entities. HOPZ needs a public notice. Many are bonded for decades. Each card says what the record shows.

The short version

TIF (tax increment financing) is the tool most of these share. The value of an area is frozen at a "base year," and the new property or sales tax collected as the area grows goes to the project instead of to schools, the county, cities and special districts for up to 25 to 45 years. A PID is different: it is its own government that bills property owners inside it directly.

Every tool, by type

2007 · S.B. 232 · Killpack / Dee
MIDA (Military Installation Development Authority)

As enacted, MIDA may "receive and use up to 75% of tax increment for up to 25 years" and issue bonds. Later bills added more powers (see S.B. 233 and S.B. 169 below).

Public vote: No. MIDA's board decides. Unelected-Power

2005 · Utah Code 63N-2-104 · bill number not yet confirmed
EDTIF / REDTIF tax credits

A refundable state tax credit for companies, paid only after they perform. It is worth up to 30% of the new state revenue a project brings in, and up to 50% under the rural version (REDTIF). Awards are approved by the GO Utah board.

Public vote: No. For balance, the credit is post-performance: nothing is paid in advance.

2015 · H.B. 362 · Rep. Johnny Anderson / Sen. Alvin Jackson
Fuel tax increase

Changed motor and special fuel taxes to raise transportation money. House 44–29, Senate 20–8. Signed March 27, 2015.

Public vote: No.

2016 · Title 17C recodified (Laws of Utah 2016, ch. 350) · bill number not yet confirmed
CRA (Community Reinvestment Area), formerly RDA

A city's or county's own TIF tool. The project area plan and the share of increment taken must be approved by the agency board and the local taxing entities. Usually runs 20 to 25 years.

Public vote: No. Taxing entities' boards approve. Amended again in 2018 by H.B. 15 (Handy / Harper).

2018 · S.B. 234 · Sen. Jerry Stevenson / Rep. Francis Gibson
UIPA (Utah Inland Port Authority)

As passed, the authority could "receive up to 100% of the property tax differential for up to 25 years, as determined by the board." Later bills changed the terms; the Tooele Valley project area takes 75%. Senate 23–4, House 61–11. Signed March 16, 2018.

Public vote: No. Counting the Jobs

2018 · Transportation Reinvestment Zone · bill number not yet confirmed
TRZ (Transportation Reinvestment Zone)

Lets governments capture tax increment to pay for transportation projects. Folded into the 2026 investment-zones chapter by S.B. 39.

Public vote: No.

2019 · S.B. 228 · Sen. Dan McCay / Rep. Jim Dunnigan
PIDs (Public Infrastructure Districts)

A development can form its own government, issue bonds and bill property owners inside it. Senate 26–0, House 66–1. Signed April 1, 2019. Statewide: $5.267 billion in PID bonds (State Auditor Tina Cannon, Aug. 7, 2026). S.B. 179 (2020, McCay) let MIDA create PIDs.

Public vote: No. Cities and counties create them; early boards are usually developer-appointed. PIDS-Utah

2019 (special session) · S.B. 2001 · Sen. Lyle Hillyard / Rep. Francis Gibson
Tax restructuring, including a higher food tax

Senate 20–7, House 43–27, signed Dec. 18, 2019. Repealed after a citizen referendum petition gathered about 152,000 signatures.

Public vote: Not on the bill itself. Citizens used a referendum to stop it.

2021 · S.B. 233 · Sen. Jerry Stevenson / Rep. Val Peterson
MIDA's PIDs get the power to levy property tax

MIDA's subsidiary PIDs may levy property taxes directly. Also added a conflict-of-interest exception and open-meetings and records exceptions. Senate 27–0, House 67–1. Signed March 22, 2021.

Public vote: No.

2021 · S.B. 217 · Sen. Wayne Harper / Rep. Stephen Handy
HTRZ (Housing and Transit Reinvestment Zone)

Captures up to 80% of the new tax above the base year near transit stations, for up to 25 years within a 45-year window, and "compels participation by other property taxing entities" (GO Utah 2024 chart). Senate 19–9, House 57–10. Signed March 22, 2021. Amended by S.B. 140 (2022, Harper).

Public vote: No. A state HTRZ committee approves.

2024 · S.B. 268 · Sen. Wayne Harper / Rep. Calvin Musselman
FHIZ (First Home Investment Zone)

Captures up to 60% of each taxing entity's increment for up to 25 years within a 45-year window. Senate 22–4, House 55–18. Signed March 21, 2024.

Public vote: No. The HTRZ committee approves.

2024 · S.B. 168 · Sen. Lincoln Fillmore / Rep. Stephen Whyte
HOPZ (Home Ownership Promotion Zone)

Created in S.B. 168, "Affordable Building Amendments." Captures up to 60% of each taxing entity's increment for up to 15 years. The city council designates the zone by resolution. Senate 25–0, House 68–1. Signed March 19, 2024.

Public vote: No. A class A public notice and HTRZ committee approval are required. Sen. Fillmore is also the primary sponsor of S.J.R. 2 (Amendment B).

2024 · H.B. 13 · Rep. Jim Dunnigan / Sen. Kirk Cullimore
IFD (Infrastructure Financing District)

Special assessment bonds for infrastructure, with terms of up to 30 years. House 74–0, Senate 24–3. Signed March 19, 2024.

Public vote: No. Property owners or developers petition, through the city's normal process.

2024 · H.B. 562 · Rep. Ryan Wilcox / Sen. Lincoln Fillmore
UFAIR (Utah Fairpark Area Investment and Restoration District)

A new district for the Fairpark and stadium area. It can levy an energy sales and use tax of up to 6%, a telecom tax of up to 3.5%, and an accommodations tax of up to 15%. It can also collect enhanced property tax revenue for up to 35 years. House 51–21, Senate 25–4. Signed March 19, 2024.

Public vote: No. Sen. Fillmore was the Senate sponsor.

2024 · S.B. 272 · Sen. Dan McCay / Rep. Jon Hawkins
Capital City Revitalization Zone: up to 0.5% sales tax

Lets Salt Lake City levy a sales tax of up to 0.5% for the Delta Center and downtown district. Senate 21–7, House 50–20. Signed March 19, 2024. The city council later approved the zone.

Public vote: No.

2024 · S.B. 169 · Sen. Jerry Stevenson / Rep. Val Peterson
MIDA 15% accommodations tax

Added a 15% accommodations tax with no end date. Senate 20–4, House 65–2. Signed March 21, 2024.

Public vote: No.

2025 · S.B. 26 · Sen. Wayne Harper / Rep. Jim Dunnigan
Convention Center Reinvestment Zone (Salt Palace)

Allows 100% of local and county tax increments and 50% of the state sales tax increment for 30 years, to repay Salt Palace bonds (KSL). KSL reported the bill "wouldn't raise city, county or state taxes." Floor votes not yet read.

Added Oct. 17, 2026, quote check: in the KSL story (March 11, 2025), "wouldn't raise city, county or state taxes" is KSL's summary of what Dunnigan said; his direct quote there is that the bill "self-funds the project through existing taxes." (KSL)

Public vote: No.

2025 · S.B. 333 · Sen. Jerry Stevenson
Sporting-venue tax vehicle

A standalone tax tool for sporting venues. It is being used in Summit County's proposed Impacted Communities Tax. Floor votes are in the Hive case file.

Public vote: No. Bill-Tracker

2025 · S.B. 316 · Sen. Jerry Stevenson
MIDA may spend project money outside the project area

Let MIDA review its own environmental impact and spend project revenue outside the project area. Not a new tax, but it widens where the captured money can go.

Public vote: No.

2026 · S.B. 39 · Sen. Wayne Harper / Rep. Stephen Whyte
All the investment zones moved into one chapter

Moved HTRZ, FHIZ, HOPZ, both convention center zones, the Capital City zone and TRZ into one chapter of code (63N-23). Effective May 6, 2026.

2026 · H.B. 507 · Rep. Calvin Roberts / Sen. Kirk Cullimore
RSDZ created; four zone types closed to new zones after 2027

Creates the Regionally Significant Development Zone (RSDZ), which can capture property tax increment. No new HTRZ, FHIZ, convention center zone or HOPZ "may … be created after January 1, 2028"; existing zones keep running. Also creates an optional County Energy Excise Tax (details not yet read), bars local incentives for large data centers with limited exceptions, and requires PID costs to be disclosed when a home is sold.

For balance: the PID disclosure and the data center limit are new protections.

2026 · S.B. 206 · Sen. Wayne Harper / Rep. R. Neil Walter
New TIF reporting system (STATS)

Creates a statewide reporting system for tax increment. The final version lets a TIF entity disclose "within 30 days after" its authorization meeting; an earlier draft required disclosure before.

2026 · S.B. 242 · Sen. Wayne Harper / Rep. Kay Christofferson
The path to Weber County's 0.20% sales tax

Section 9 of this transportation bill amended Utah Code 59-12-2220. Weber County used it to adopt the "5th 5th" 0.20% sales tax, about $13.1 million a year, effective Oct. 1, 2026.

Public vote: No. The County Commission voted. CASE 2026-STAX-01

2026 · H.B. 575 · Rep. Calvin Roberts / Sen. Brady Brammer
Gas tax cut, alternative-fuel tax raised

Gas tax was set to a flat $0.319 a gallon for July 1 to Dec. 31, 2026, while the tax on CNG, LNG and hydrogen rose from a 16.5¢ cap to 21.2¢.

Public vote: No.

For balance: in the same years the Legislature cut the income tax from 4.95% to 4.45% (2022–2026). Items marked "not yet confirmed" will be updated once the bill is found. Taxation for Beginners explains each tool in more depth.

The collective questions

  • Power: A moves the notice method from the Constitution to statute, where the Legislature can change it by majority. B raises the vote citizens need for tax initiatives only.
  • Transparency: A ends the newspaper requirement that voided two 2024 amendments; how notice will be given instead is set later by statute. B's trigger is "includes" and "current law" is undefined.
  • Financial accountability: Neither amendment changes how the Legislature, local governments, MIDA, UIPA or PID boards raise taxes or issue debt.
  • Environmental impact: No direct effect; initiatives that fund resource protection with a tax would face B's 60% bar.
  • Community impact: Both change how residents take part in constitutional and initiative decisions; neither touches decisions made without a public vote.

Initiative, referendum, amendment: words that sound alike, but aren't

Initiatives, referendums, amendments and bills all end up as "votes," but they start in different places and need different numbers to pass. Amendment B changes only one row.

Bill (statute)

An ordinary law.

Who starts it
A legislator
How it's decided
Both houses vote, then the governor signs or vetoes
Vote needed
Majority of each house
Does Amendment B change it?
No
Can the Legislature change it later?
Yes, by another bill

Veto override

Passing a bill again after the governor rejects it.

Who starts it
The Legislature
How it's decided
Both houses vote again
Vote needed
Two-thirds of each house (Utah Const. Art. VII, Sec. 8)
Does Amendment B change it?
No
Can the Legislature change it later?
—

Citizen initiative

A new law written by citizens.

Who starts it
Citizens
How it's decided
Signatures from 8% of active voters in 26 of 29 Senate districts (about 140,000), then a statewide vote
Vote needed
Simple majority today. 60% if B passes and it includes a tax increase as S.J.R. 2 defines it. Wildlife initiatives: two-thirds.
Does Amendment B change it?
YES. This is the only one it changes.
Can the Legislature change it later?
Yes. Since July 2024, a change that impairs a government-reform initiative must be narrowly tailored to a compelling government interest.

Referendum

Citizens challenge a law the Legislature just passed.

Who starts it
Citizens
How it's decided
Signatures from 8% of active voters statewide and in 15 of 29 Senate districts, filed within 40 days after the session; then a statewide vote
Vote needed
Simple majority
Does Amendment B change it?
No
Can the Legislature change it later?
Yes. In January 2020 the Legislature repealed its own 2019 tax law (S.B. 2001), including the higher food tax, after a referendum qualified.

Constitutional amendment

A change to the Utah Constitution. Amendments A and B are these.

Who starts it
Only the Legislature
How it's decided
Two-thirds of each house puts it on the ballot; then a statewide vote
Vote needed
Simple majority of voters
Does Amendment B change it?
B is one
Can the Legislature change it later?
Only with another amendment and another statewide vote

Sources: vote.utah.gov, "Instructions for a Statewide Initiative" and "Instructions for a Statewide Referendum"; Utah Const. Art. VI, Art. VII Sec. 8, Art. XXIII; S.J.R. 2 (2025); League of Women Voters v. Utah Legislature (July 11, 2024).

Every tool, and who gets to use it

Tools voters and citizens have

  • Statewide initiative: write a new law and put it to a vote. The only tool Amendment B changes.
  • Statewide referendum: challenge a law the Legislature just passed. Majority vote.
  • Local initiative and referendum: the same tools for city and county laws, with signature counts based on local voters. Amendment B covers statewide initiatives only.
  • Voting on constitutional amendments: every amendment, including A and B, needs voter approval.
  • Bond elections: general obligation bonds go to voters. Lease revenue bonds and PID bonds do not.
  • Truth in Taxation hearings: residents can speak before a property-tax increase. It's a hearing, not a vote.

Tools the Legislature and local governments have

  • Pass a bill: majority of each house, including tax increases.
  • Override a veto: two-thirds of each house.
  • Propose a constitutional amendment: two-thirds of each house, then voters decide.
  • Call itself into special session: two-thirds of each house for a "persistent fiscal crisis, war, natural disaster, or emergency in the affairs of the state" (2018 amendment; used Aug. 2024).
  • Amend or repeal an initiative afterward: by majority, with the 2024 limit for government-reform initiatives.
  • Repeal its own law before a referendum vote: as with the 2019 tax law in January 2020.
  • Local governments: raise property tax after a Truth in Taxation hearing, adopt some sales taxes by council or commission vote, and create PIDs, CRAs and other zones (see Tax tools).

Sources: vote.utah.gov; Utah Const. Art. VII Sec. 8, Art. XXIII; KUER, April 22, 2025, and Utah News Dispatch, Aug. 2024, on special sessions; CASE 2026-STAX-01 (Weber County 0.20% sales tax).

How each tool has been used in Utah, and why that route

Real examples from the record. "Why this route" uses the reasons stated at the time; where no reason was stated, it explains what the rules required.

Bill

H.B. 362 (2015): fuel tax increase

What happened
Passed House 44–29, Senate 20–8; signed March 27, 2015.
Why this route
Raising a tax is the Legislature's own power, so it only needed a majority of each house. The bill's title was "Transportation Infrastructure Funding."

Veto override

H.B. 11 (2022): transgender students in school sports

What happened
Gov. Cox vetoed it; the Legislature met in a veto override session on March 25, 2022 and overrode it, House 56–18, Senate 21–8.

An override was the only way to make it law after the veto. Reported reasons for the veto included concern for transgender students' welfare and legal costs for school districts.

Prop 2 → H.B. 3001 (Dec. 3, 2018); Prop 3 → S.B. 96 (Feb. 2019); Prop 4 → S.B. 200 (2020)

What happened
Prop 2 was replaced in a special session four weeks after the vote (Senate 22–4). Prop 3 was changed in the next session.
Why this route
Prop 2: supporters and opponents agreed in October 2018 to a compromise law that would pass "regardless of the outcome" (Marijuana Policy Project, via KUTV). Prop 3: the stated reason was cost; Sen. Allen Christensen said the sales tax would raise $88 million in year one against $105 million needed (KSL). Prop 4: the Utah Supreme Court ruled in 2024 that government-reform initiatives are protected from unlimited repeal.

KUTV, Dec. 3, 2018; KSL, Jan. 2019; KJZZ

Referendum

S.B. 2001 (2019): tax restructuring, including a higher food tax

What happened
Passed in a December 2019 special session. A referendum gathered enough signatures; the Legislature repealed the law itself in January 2020, before the vote.
Why this route
The law had already passed, so citizens used a referendum, the tool for challenging a new law, instead of an initiative.

le.utah.gov; KSL, Jan. 28, 2020

Referendum

H.B. 267 (2025): ban on public-employee collective bargaining

What happened
Campaign reported more than 320,000 signers. The Legislature repealed the law in a special session on Dec. 9, 2025 (House 60–9, Senate 26–1) instead of facing a November 2026 vote.
Why this route
Primary sponsor Rep. Jordan Teuscher said the bill "has been overshadowed by misinformation and unnecessary division" and the repeal would "lower the temperature."

Ballotpedia News, Dec. 11, 2025; KPCW

Constitutional amendment

2018 Amendment C: lets the Legislature call itself into special session

What happened
Passed with about 63% of the vote; took effect Jan. 1, 2019.
Why this route
Changing the balance between the Legislature and the governor required changing the Constitution. Then-Speaker Brad Wilson said it put "an equal branch of government on equal footing"; Gov. Herbert opposed it, warning lawmakers "can define 'emergency' themselves."

KUER, Nov. 23, 2018

Special session the Legislature called itself

August 2024: placing 2024 Amendment D on the ballot

What happened
Two-thirds of each house agreed to convene, using the 2018 Amendment C power. Amendment D was later voided by the Utah Supreme Court.
Why this route
The session came after the July 2024 Prop 4 ruling limited the Legislature's power over reform initiatives; an amendment was the route to change that limit.

Utah News Dispatch, Aug. 2024; FOX 13

Bond election

Weber County jail bond, 2023

What happened
Voters turned down a $98 million general obligation bond.
Why this route
General obligation bonds must go to voters. For contrast: Alpine School District issued $201 million in lease revenue bonds in 2025 with no vote, after S.B. 188.

CASE 2026-STAX-01; Bill-Tracker

Truth in Taxation hearing

Weber County 7.25% property-tax increase (for 2024)

What happened
Hearing held Nov. 28, 2023; the commission adopted the increase. No public vote.
Why this route
A property-tax increase above the certified rate requires notice and a hearing, not an election. Coverage at the time described it as about $3.9 million for worker pay and inflation.

Weber County Commission minutes; KSL NewsRadio, Oct. 25, 2023

Local sales tax by commission vote

Weber County 0.20% "5th 5th" sales tax (2026)

What happened
Adopted unanimously by the County Commission; about $13.1 million a year, effective Oct. 1, 2026.
Why this route
State law (amended by S.B. 242, 2026) lets the county adopt it by vote. Commissioner Gage Froerer called it "an alternative to relying solely on property taxes."

Weber County, July 1, 2026 release; CASE 2026-STAX-01

Words to know

Tap a card to flip it.

Publication Clausetap to define
The part of Article XXIII, Sec. 1 that says how proposed amendments must be published before the vote. Amendment A rewrites it.
Statutetap to define
An ordinary law passed by the Legislature and signed by the governor. Easier to change than the Constitution.
Initiativetap to define
A law proposed by citizens and adopted by voters.
Referendumtap to define
A citizen challenge to a law already passed, put to voters by petition.
Voidedtap to define
Ruled invalid by a court, so the votes do not take effect.
PIDtap to define
Public Infrastructure District: a district that can levy assessments and issue bonds inside its boundary.
Truth in Taxationtap to define
Utah's process: when values rise, the property-tax rate falls automatically unless the taxing body holds a public hearing to keep more revenue. No public vote.
Tax increment (TIF)tap to define
New property tax from rising values inside a project area, redirected to that project instead of the usual taxing bodies.
Class A noticetap to define
Utah's standard public notice: posted on the Utah Public Notice Website, on the agency's website, and in a public place. No newspaper required.
Veto overridetap to define
When the Legislature passes a bill again after the governor rejects it. Takes two-thirds of each house in Utah.
Supermajoritytap to define
Any required vote higher than a simple majority, such as 60% or two-thirds.
Government-reform initiativetap to define
An initiative that changes how government works, like Prop 4's redistricting commission. Since 2024, the Legislature can't impair one without a compelling reason.
