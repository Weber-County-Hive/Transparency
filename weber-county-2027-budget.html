What to Watch in Weber County's 2027 Budget

General Fund taxes are budgeted at $81.2 million for 2027, up from $68.3 million in the 2026 approved budget. Most of the jump is the new 0.20% sales tax. Here is what the county's own budget book shows, and what to ask before the Nov. 24 hearing.

The Weber County Hive · Independent Accountability Journalism

CASE 2026-BUD-01 · Posted Nov. 5, 2026

Short version
  • Weber County posted its 2027 tentative budget on Oct. 28, 2026. The public hearing is Nov. 24 at 6 p.m.; the commission adopts the final budget on Dec. 15.
  • General Fund taxes: $67.2 million (2025 approved), $68.3 million (2026 approved), $81.2 million (2027 tentative).
  • Two new lines carry most of the jump: Transit Sales Tax - County ($3.2 million) and Transit Sales Tax - District ($6.5 million). They match the 0.20% sales tax that took effect Oct. 1.
  • The same page adds $9.7 million in new transfers out. Capital Projects gains $6.5 million and Paramedic $1.5 million.
  • A new $34 million construction line covers the Weber Center remodel and a jail medical and mental health expansion. The county's plan says funding is not yet identified for all of its projects.
  • Several funds are budgeted to spend more than they bring in, drawing on savings, as they did in 2025.

Explain this simply

Every fall, the county writes a plan for how much money it expects to take in next year and how it will spend it. That plan is the tentative budget. People can speak about it at a public hearing before the commissioners vote on the final version in December.

This year's plan includes money from a new sales tax that started Oct. 1. It also plans to spend some savings on big building projects and roads. This page shows those lines, with the page number for each, so you can check them and ask about them.

The new sales tax shows up: $9.7 million

General Fund taxes, budget by budget

From the 2025 final, 2026 final and 2027 tentative budget books, General Fund "Taxes" line.

What makes up the 2027 jump (vs. 2026 estimated)

Page 62. Together these add to the $11.24 million increase in the Taxes line.

In plain wordsThe county plans on about $13 million more in taxes next year than this year's budget planned. Most of it comes from the new sales tax: a little extra on things people buy in Weber County, which started Oct. 1.

Most of the jump in General Fund taxes comes from two lines that appear for the first time in 2027, on page 62 of the budget book:

Line (page 62)2026 estimated2027 tentative
Transit Sales Tax - County$0$3,200,000
Transit Sales Tax - District$0$6,500,000
Total new$9,700,000

The budget book does not explain these lines. They match the 0.20% county option sales tax the commission adopted June 23, 2026, which took effect Oct. 1. Other tax lines grow more slowly: current property taxes go from $41.09 million (2026 estimated) to $41.92 million, about 2%, and the ¼% sales tax from $20.76 million to $21.54 million.

Why the county holds the transit share. Under Utah Code 59-12-2220, as amended in 2026, a county like Weber splits the tax 0.10% to transit, 0.05% to cities and 0.05% to the county. For the first few years, the transit share is transferred to the county "for a purpose determined by the county legislative body." That lasts until three years after at least three counties have imposed the tax, or June 30, 2030, whichever comes first. After that, half goes to the state's commuter rail account and half stays with the county for transit uses.

Does this take money from city budgets? No. Cities still get their own 0.05% of the new tax, split by population and where sales happen. The Hive's city-by-city table shows each city's estimated share. The share the county is holding for now is the transit share, which the law would otherwise send to transit. It is new money from shoppers, not money taken out of a city budget.

This confirms the figure the Hive's sales tax case file already relies on: about $9.8 million a year under county control, not the "$2 million to $3 million" reported in June.

The no-swap rule. The same law says this money may not replace General Fund money the county had already budgeted for transportation or transit when the tax took effect. The law also made a ballot question optional, and none was held.

In plain wordsThink of the county's main checking account. New sales tax money comes in, and almost the same amount goes right out to other accounts: mostly the building-projects account and the ambulance account.

A $34 million construction line

The Capital Projects Fund adds a new $34,000,000 "Construction Project" line for 2027 (page 161). The county's capital plan (page 199) names the two projects behind it:

Project20272028Total
Weber Center Remodel$20,000,000$13,900,000$33,900,000
Jail Medical and Mental Health Expansion$14,000,000$15,000,000$29,000,000

The plan says "funding has not yet been identified for all of these projects." The fund brings in $10.2 million in 2027 and is budgeted to spend $36.98 million, with the rest coming from its savings. The plan lists $209.9 million in requested projects through 2030 and beyond, including a Fairgrounds renovation ($87 million) and a Northwest library branch ($48.1 million). Its 2027 column, $36.98 million, matches the Capital Projects Fund's 2027 spending exactly. Voters turned down a $98 million jail bond in 2023.

In plain wordsThe county plans to spend $34 million next year to fix up the Weber Center, its main office building, and to add medical and mental health space at the jail. Some of the money for these projects has not been found yet.

Who decided, and whether the public voted

None of these items went to a public vote. State law did not require one for any of them. Each was decided by elected or appointed boards in public meetings.

ItemWho decidedPublic vote?

The one time voters were asked: the jail. In November 2023, Weber County voters rejected a bond of up to $98 million, 57.6% to 42.4% in preliminary results. It would have paid for jail upgrades, including a 48-bed section for inmates with mental health and medical needs, and a new sheriff's office building (Standard-Examiner).

Afterward, Sheriff Ryan Arbon said the jail's medical and mental health problems still needed solutions, and Commissioner Gage Froerer said a future plan would likely be scaled back. In April 2024 the county looked at a smaller $30.8 million plan focused on medical and mental health beds, and Froerer said he did not want to raise property taxes to pay for it (KSL). When the commission adopted the sales tax in June 2026, Froerer told KSL most of the money would likely go to the sheriff's office, including jail mental health space. The 2027 budget now lists a $29 million jail medical and mental health expansion.

The 2023 bond was a larger package than the current project. Voters were not asked about the smaller project or about the sales tax that helps pay for capital projects.

In plain wordsNobody had a public vote on any of this. The law did not require one. The last time voters were asked about the jail, in 2023, most said no to a bigger plan.

Each gap is paid from that fund's savings. From the 2027 tentative budget fund pages.

Inside Transportation Development, the larger sub-fund lists $29.25 million in "Special Projects" for 2027, and a second sub-fund lists $11.26 million more (pages 115–116). In plain wordsSome county funds plan to spend more than they take in next year, using money they saved before. That works for a while, like spending from a savings account, but the savings can run out.

The tax increment layer the budget doesn't show

The county has also agreed to let part of its future property tax growth in certain areas go to project areas. Two things are often misunderstood:

  1. The tax is collected. Property owners in these areas pay the full tax rate, and the county treasurer collects every dollar. The increment share is then allocated to the project area instead of the county's budget. Taxpayers still pay it; it is redirected after collection, not "never collected."
  2. Only the agreed share, for the agreed term. It is not "all the growth goes to the project area." At Nordic Village, 75% of the county's increment and 50% of the school, fire and water districts' increment go to the project, for 15 years. In West Weber, 75% of the tax differential goes to UIPA for 25 years. The rest still flows to the taxing entities.

It adds up across the county, including Ogden

Nordic Village and West Weber are not the only project areas. Every property in Ogden also pays county taxes, so when an Ogden project area takes a share of the growth, part of it is the county's share.

At Wildcat Square, the county would give 50% of its share from six county funds: general ($216,001), bonds, library ($79,442), health, paramedics ($19,721) and flood control. The county's budget book does not add up these project-area commitments in one place. The figures above are the closest public total the Hive has found.

The 2027 budget book does not name Nordic Village, West Weber, the Inland Port or any PID. The one place increment appears is the county's redevelopment fund (page 119): increment property taxes of $1.23 million budgeted for 2026, now estimated at $2.29 million, and $2.3 million for 2027. That fund lists $2.25 million in "Incentive Payments" and $3.25 million in "Contributions" for 2027, without naming who receives them.

No single public document adds up what the county has committed in future growth across all of these areas. Whether these commitments affect the funds above is a question the records reviewed here do not answer.

In plain wordsWhen a neighborhood grows, its property taxes grow too. In these special areas, owners still pay their whole tax bill. Then part of the extra money from the growth goes to pay for roads, pipes and other things in that area for a set number of years, instead of going to the county. When the years are up, it all goes back to the county.

Limits an auditor would point out:

  • Mixed budget stages. The 2027 figures are tentative and can change by Dec. 15. Comparisons to 2026 use the approved budget or the county's 2026 estimate, as labeled.
  • The 2025 column in the fund-gap table comes from the Hive's earlier sales tax page. It was a tentative budget, and it may not count transfers the same way as the 2027 figures here.
  • Fund savings not checked. A gap only matters next to how much a fund has saved. Those balances are not on these pages.
  • Transfers are not traced. The budget does not say which fund sends the $1.5 million to Paramedic or the $10.2 million to Capital Projects. The $8 million match is a fact about the totals, not a link the county states.
  • Open money question. The jail plan in December 2025 counted on about $10 million in opioid settlement money. Whether that money sits inside the $29 million capital line or in another fund is not shown.

Words to know

Tentative budget

The county's proposed budget for next year, posted for public comment before the final vote.

Fund balance

Money a fund has saved from past years. "Uses of fund balance" means spending some of those savings.

Transfers to other funds

Money moved from one county fund to another, such as from the General Fund to Capital Projects.

Tax increment

The growth in property tax above a frozen base year inside a project area. Owners pay the full rate; the agreed share of the growth is then allocated to the project area for the agreed term.

"5th 5th" sales tax

The nickname for the 0.20% county option sales and use tax in Utah Code §59-12-2220.
