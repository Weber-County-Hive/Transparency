Lost Ideals: What Utah Promised Its Voters

Utah's constitution and laws promise that power belongs to the people, that elections are free and that government works in the open. Here is each promise in its own words, and what has changed.

The short version

  • Utah's 1895 constitution says "All political power is inherent in the people." In 1900, voters added the power to pass laws themselves by initiative and to send laws to a public vote by referendum, 71% to 29%.
  • Utah's own statutes promise open meetings and "easy and reasonable access" to public records, and favor access "when... countervailing interests are of equal weight."
  • Since 2011, the Legislature has narrowed records access several times, changed or replaced all three 2018 voter initiatives, limited some local referendums, and gained the power to call itself into session.
  • Voters and courts have pushed back: the 2011 records law was repealed within three weeks, and the Utah Supreme Court said in 2024 that the people's reform power is "protected from government infringement."

What Utah promised, in its own words

Utah Constitution, Article I, Section 2 (1895)
"All political power is inherent in the people; and all free governments are founded on their authority for their equal protection and benefit, and they have the right to alter or reform their government as the public welfare may require." (le.utah.gov)
Article I, Section 17 (1895)
"All elections shall be free, and no power, civil or military, shall at any time interfere to prevent the free exercise of the right of suffrage." (le.utah.gov)
Article IV, Section 1 (1895)
"Both male and female citizens of this State shall enjoy equally all civil, political and religious rights and privileges." (le.utah.gov)
Open and Public Meetings Act, Utah Code 52-4-102
"The state, its agencies and political subdivisions, exist to aid in the conduct of the people's business... take their actions openly; and conduct their deliberations openly." (le.utah.gov)
Government Records Access and Management Act (GRAMA), Utah Code 63G-2-102
"Promote the public's right of easy and reasonable access to unrestricted public records... favor public access when, in the application of this act, countervailing interests are of equal weight." (le.utah.gov)

At the 1895 convention, delegate Heber Wells said it was "very pertinent to provide that all political power is inherent in the people" (as quoted in the Utah Supreme Court's 2024 opinion). Equal suffrage passed the convention 75–14 on April 5, 1895 (Utah Women's History).

1900: the people's lawmaking power

Utah was the second state, after South Dakota, to give voters initiative and referendum.

  • The vote: Nov. 6, 1900. Yes 19,219 (71.17%), no 7,786 (28.83%) (Ballotpedia).
  • Who pushed it: Rep. Sherman S. Smith of Ogden, the Legislature's lone Populist, is described as the person most responsible (Deseret News, 2020).
  • The text then: voters "may initiate any desired legislation" or require any law, except those passed by two-thirds, "to be submitted to the voters of the State before such law shall take effect." That wording has changed very little; the main change was a 1998 two-thirds rule for wildlife initiatives (2024 UT 21, fn. 28).
  • The wait: the enabling law came years later (1917 per Ballotpedia; being confirmed). Its first version required signers to sign in front of an officer who could give oaths. The first statewide initiative to pass was in 1960.
Both sides in 1900. For: with adoption, "the people of the State would be rulers of the State, which they were not now" (Kate S. Hilliard, Salt Lake Tribune, Oct. 28, 1900). Against: the amendment would "cause a great departure from our legislative system" (Deseret Evening News, Nov. 3, 1900). Both as quoted in 2024 UT 21.

The promise and what changed

Voters can make laws (Art. VI, Sec. 1)All three 2018 initiatives changed or replaced (H.B. 3001, S.B. 96, S.B. 200). In 2024, lawmakers proposed Amendment D to let them change any initiative.The Utah Supreme Court (July 2024): the people's reform power is "protected from government infringement." Courts voided Amendment D.
Voters can send a law to a vote (referendum)Some local land use laws made not referable (H.B. 462, 2022; S.B. 199, 2023). A referendum is void if lawmakers repeal the law first (H.B. 75, 2020).Referendum drives led lawmakers to repeal laws in 2011, 2020 and 2025.
"Easy and reasonable access" to records (GRAMA)2011 HB477 restricted records access. 2025: the seven-member State Records Committee replaced by one director the governor appoints (S.B. 277); court costs for winning records appeals limited to "bad faith" cases (H.B. 69); university president finalists kept private (S.B. 282). New exemptions in H.B. 61, H.B. 491 and H.B. 575.HB477 repealed March 25, 2011 (House 60–3, Senate 19–5). The Society of Professional Journalists gave the Legislature its 2025 "Black Hole Award."

Each side, in its own words

The court (2024). "The people's constitutional right to alter or reform their government is protected from government infringement" (2024 UT 21).
Legislative leaders. On the Amendment D ruling, Senate President Stuart Adams and Speaker Mike Schultz called it "unprecedented and troubling" (Deseret News, 9/25/2024). Later, Schultz: "I made some mistakes, and I'll own that." Adams: "we all can do better" (Utah News Dispatch, 11/8/2024).
Voters' groups. Katharine Biele, League of Women Voters of Utah: "I hope the legislature understands they represent us." Emma Petty Addams, Mormon Women for Ethical Government: "constitutional checks and balances exist for a reason" (Deseret News, 9/25/2024).
A legislator on raising the bar for lawmakers. Rep. Norm Thurston (R) wrote in a public comment on Oct. 9, 2026: "I do agree that the Legislature should also have a higher bar." He said that if Amendment B passed, he would pursue a two-thirds vote for tax increases in both chambers. Amendment B failed.
On records (2011 and 2025). Gov. Herbert on repealing HB477: "It was the right thing to do as a first step to restore public confidence" (KSL). Rep. Stephanie Gricius said H.B. 69 "does not restrict access"; the Utah Media Coalition said it "weakens GRAMA" (KSL, 2/19/2025).
