Wells at the Top: Powder Mountain's Water

A new application seeks 1,000 acre-feet from nine wells in the Powder Mountain area, with protests due Oct. 7. An earlier one, for five new mountaintop wells and snowmaking, had its hearing in February 2024 and is currently listed in final review, and the public district behind it has owners in the resort's own development on its board.

Explain this simply

Water in Utah is owned as rights, and older rights come first when there isn't enough.

Powder Mountain's public water district asked the state to combine rights, drill five new wells on top of the mountain and add snowmaking.

Its plan would also cut back water it had agreed to supply to Wolf Creek Irrigation, whose rights date to 1861. Five neighbors protested.

The state held a hearing in February 2024; its tracker still lists the application in final review. In August 2026 the resort's developer filed a second application, for 1,000 acre-feet from nine wells, with protests due Oct. 7. Meanwhile, the district's board, which includes owners in the resort's development, voted to hand all its operations to a contractor, with the resort as the expected bidder.

Acre-foot

The water it takes to cover one acre one foot deep: about 325,851 gallons.

Exchange application

A request to the state to take water at a different place or way, in return for replacing it elsewhere. Numbers start with "E."

Mitigation

Water a user agrees to supply so its new use doesn't hurt other rights holders.

Senior right

An older water right. When supply falls short, older rights are filled first.

Snowmaking additive

A product added to snowmaking water to help it freeze at warmer temperatures. The most common, Snomax, is made from a protein of inactivated bacteria.

Protest

A written objection to a water application, filed with the Division of Water Rights by the deadline in the notice.

Powder Mountain's growth needs water as well as fire trucks. The public district that supplies the resort has asked the state to change how it uses its water, including five new wells at the top of the mountain and water for snowmaking. Neighbors who hold older water rights filed protests, and more than two and a half years later the state's tracker still lists that application in final review. Now a second application, filed by the resort's developer, seeks 1,000 acre-feet from nine wells. Protests on it are due Oct. 7, 2026.

In late November 2023, the Powder Mountain Water and Sewer Improvement District filed an exchange application with the Utah Division of Water Rights. The Standard-Examiner reported it would:

  • Combine Powder Mountain's and Summit's water rights, giving the resort "over 600 acre feet to tap."
  • Include "the drilling of five new mountaintop wells."
  • Add snowmaking, "something new for Powder Mountain."
  • Change a 2016 mitigation agreement with Wolf Creek Irrigation Co., "reducing the diversion value of its 15 Wolf Creek shares by about 38%."

The 2016 agreement had required Summit to buy 15 Wolf Creek shares for mitigation and build a 20-acre-foot reservoir to supplement Wolf Creek's low-water flows. Powder Mountain said it "made every effort to ensure no other water right holders are harmed" by the application.

Who protested

The Standard-Examiner's February 2024 reporting identified five protestors: Wolf Creek Irrigation Co., Wolf Creek Water & Sewer Improvement District, Eden Water Works, Gay Browning of Bar B Ranch, and Middle Fork Irrigation Co. The hearing was held Feb. 22, 2024, at the North Ogden City Offices. The protest-and-hearing process is what state law provides, and it was followed.

Added Oct. 17, 2026: The state's online correspondence log for E6560 (read as page text on Oct. 17, 2026) lists 11 entries labeled as protests, from 9 filers, between Dec. 21, 2023, and March 14, 2024. Besides the groups named above, they include PacifiCorp, a business, a family trust and individual residents. Two of the filings, from Eden Water Works and the Wolf Creek Water & Sewer district, are marked late. On March 19, 2024, Middle Fork Irrigation Co., Bar B Ranch and the family trust filed a protest withdrawal; Bar B Ranch sent a letter of concern two days later. The log's last entry is dated April 4, 2024, and it shows no decision. E6560 correspondence log.

What the records show about supply

The records reviewed describe a valley where water is already limited:

  • Wolf Creek Irrigation Co. holds senior rights dating to 1861 and an allocation of 9.85 cubic feet per second, but receives about 4 cubic feet per second in summer months, the Standard-Examiner reported in 2024.
  • Eden Crossing had an earlier water application denied by the state in March 2025, per the Hive's Eden Crossing files. That is a different filing from its current exchange application, E6743.
  • Liberty Pipeline Company says on its own website that it isn't issuing new shares, citing drought and repairs. Added Oct. 17, 2026: posts on the company's site dated June 30 and Aug. 2, 2026, ask shareholders to conserve water during "this year of severe drought."

Why make snow on a powder mountain?

Powder Mountain is known for natural snow, and E6560 would bring snowmaking there for the first time. The records reviewed don't include Powder Mountain's own stated reason or how much water it plans to use for it. Here is what is on the record about snowmaking generally.

  • Why resorts do it. In a 2021 Deseret News report on Utah resorts, Utah State University researcher Patrick Belmont said resorts "know that the quality of their famously light snow is decreasing," and "what's most important to them is that there's snow on the ground." Resorts use it to open early and keep high-traffic runs covered in low-snow years.
  • How much water. The same report said Deer Valley uses "about 240 million gallons of water a season" to make snow. No comparable figure for Powder Mountain was found.
  • How much is lost. A field study near Davos, Switzerland (Wolfsperger and Grünewald, 2019) found "7 to 35±7% (mean 21%) of the consumed water was lost during snow making," with losses rising as air temperature rose. The study counts water that evaporates or sublimates, snow that blows off the slope, and unfrozen droplets that drain into the ground. Water missing from the slope isn't necessarily gone from the watershed, so the study doesn't establish a loss figure for Powder Mountain. Supporters note the water is taken when farms aren't using it and melts back into the watershed; how that works for water pumped from new mountaintop wells wasn't addressed in the records reviewed.
  • What goes in it. Some resorts add Snomax, made from a protein of inactivated bacteria, to help water freeze at warmer temperatures. France's health and environment agency, ANSES, rated its risk "nil to negligible" for the public and recommended monitoring the water's quality. Whether Powder Mountain would use any additive, and what water-quality safeguards would apply, wasn't found in the records reviewed.
What this means for the valley

Snowmaking isn't the same as water used up: most of it melts back in spring. What it means here depends on local facts the records reviewed don't show: how much is pumped, from which wells, when it melts and where it flows, and how that affects existing rights. Those are the questions the state engineer weighs on E6560 and E6863.

Who runs the water district

The Standard-Examiner reported on April 11, 2026:

  • The board chair "acknowledged that multiple board members own real estate in Powder Haven, the luxury residential development being built by the resort."
  • County Commissioner Jim Harvey, who voted on a 2017 revenue-sharing agreement, "now sits on the board of the water district."
  • On April 3, 2026, the board "voted unanimously to issue a Request for Proposals seeking a private contractor to take over all day-to-day operations," and approved the RFP and a contract of services in one motion. The district had five employees; the RFP set a 90-day transition "after which the district would have no employees."
  • "The developer now owns the ground under the district's sewer lagoons, owns the district's only functioning water source, and stands as the expected respondent to an RFP for all remaining operations."
The resort, as the district's largest customer, "could from time to time act in its own interests to the detriment of other customers."
District attorney Ari Bruening, in writing, as reported by the Standard-Examiner. He also wrote that an arrangement with the resort "would likely generate significant efficiencies."

Whether the contract was awarded, and to whom, wasn't found in the records reviewed.
