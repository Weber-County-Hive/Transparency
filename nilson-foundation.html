The short version
  1. A new charity, the Nilson Foundation, was created in Utah on July 21, 2026.
  2. Ten days later, it posted a flyer online asking people to donate — furniture, beds, household goods.
  3. The flyer had a badge on it that said "501(c)(3) NONPROFIT" and told donors their gifts were "Tax-Deductible."
  4. That badge wasn't true yet. Getting real federal tax-exempt status normally takes months, not ten days.
  5. A direct IRS records search confirms it: the Nilson Foundation has no federal 501(c)(3) determination at all.
  6. Weeks later, the claim is still up — not just on the original flyer, but on the organization's own live website, in two separate places.
A charity told donors their gifts were tax-deductible before it had any legal basis to say so — and it's still telling them that today.
Key records at a glance
  • Nilson Foundation Inc. was incorporated in Utah on July 21, 2026. Its officers are Jed Nilson (president), Mike Ostermiller (treasurer, registered agent and incorporator) and Anne Dunaway (secretary).
  • Its July 31, 2026 flyer said "501(c)(3) NONPROFIT" and "ALL DONATIONS ARE Tax-Deductible."
  • IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search checks on Aug. 31 and Sept. 3, 2026 found no 501(c)(3) determination for it.
  • The IRS search shows determinations, not pending applications. Whether a Form 1023 was filed is unconfirmed.
  • Utah's 2024 H.B. 43 lets new charities ask for gifts right away, but makes it a deceptive practice to falsely say a gift carries a tax benefit.

What the law actually says

Utah changed its charity-registration rules in 2024. It's worth being precise about what changed and what didn't, because there are really two separate questions here — only one of which is actually against the law.

What the IRS says

An organization is not legally tax-exempt under 501(c)(3) until the IRS itself issues a determination letter. Until that letter arrives, the organization is treated as a taxable entity for federal purposes. If it files Form 1023 within 27 months of its formation date and is later approved, the exemption applies retroactively back to formation — meaning early donors could eventually claim their deduction. If the application is denied, or never filed, the organization was never exempt, and any donor who already claimed a deduction based on the organization's promise risks having it disallowed by the IRS on audit.

The IRS itself has no specific penalty aimed at an organization for prematurely claiming exempt status in its own marketing. Its enforcement mechanism is about the organization's own tax liability, and the donor's deduction — not policing what a charity says in a flyer.

What Utah says

Not illegal: soliciting donations right after incorporating. Utah repealed its old pre-registration requirement effective May 1, 2024. A brand-new nonprofit is legally free to start asking for donations immediately — there's no waiting period.
The actual legal problem: a 2024 Utah law, H.B. 43, added language to the state's Consumer Sales Practices Act making it a "deceptive act or practice" to falsely indicate, in connection with a charitable solicitation, that a donation carries "an additional benefit, including a tax benefit." Utah's separate charitable-solicitation statute independently bars any "false statement of a material fact" in a solicitation. Neither the original flyer nor the current website ever uses a qualifier like "pending" — both flatly claim exempt status outright. This is where the real, enforceable penalty against the organization actually lives — not in federal tax law, but in Utah's own consumer-protection statute.

What this actually risks for donors

If a donor claims the deduction now, before any determination exists, and the Nilson Foundation's application is ultimately denied — or never filed at all — that donor would be claiming a deduction they were never entitled to. The IRS can disallow it on audit, with penalties and interest. There's no automatic mechanism where the IRS catches up and retroactively grants the credit — it's entirely on the donor to track the organization's status, and to file an amended return themselves if they already claimed it. That's the real exposure for anyone who gave based on the flyer's flat "tax-deductible" claim, rather than being told the status was pending.

What compliant language actually looks like

These are the specific claims that are problematic without an actual IRS determination:

  • "We are an IRS-approved 501(c)(3)."
  • "Your donation is tax-deductible," without explaining that approval is pending and deductibility is uncertain.
  • Displaying an invented determination date or exemption letter.
  • Claiming to appear in the IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search when the organization does not.

The accurate wording an organization in this position should use instead:

"We are a Utah nonprofit corporation and have applied for recognition under §501(c)(3). Our application is pending. Donations are not assured to be tax-deductible unless and until the IRS approves our exemption for the relevant period." — what compliant marketing language looks like

Neither the Nilson Foundation's original flyer nor its current website uses language resembling this. Both state, without qualification, that the organization is a "501(c)(3) Nonprofit" and that donations "are tax-deductible."

What penalties actually attach to this

Utah's Division of Consumer Protection can issue citations, cease-and-desist orders, and administrative fines up to $2,500 per violation — or up to $10,000 for a series of violations from the same facts. A court can add fines up to $2,000 per violation, disgorgement, injunctive relief, and civil penalties up to $5,000 for violating an order. Separately, a person who willfully violates Utah's charitable-solicitation law is guilty of a class B misdemeanor, with each day of a continuing violation counted as a separate offense.

H.B. 43's primary sponsor was Rep. A. Cory Maloy; Senate sponsor was Sen. Curtis Bramble.

Added Oct. 17, 2026: the Legislature's bill page shows H.B. 43 passed the House 69-0 on Feb. 23, 2024, and the Senate 23-0 on Feb. 29, 2024, and was signed by the governor on March 13, 2024. Its text says it takes effect May 1, 2024 (le.utah.gov).

Why this is being watched closely

This isn't the first time a newly formed, politically-connected Utah nonprofit has drawn scrutiny over how it's funded. Utah Workforce Housing Advocacy — a separate organization formed by politically connected individuals — has previously been reported on for receiving taxpayer funding routed through the Governor's Office of Economic Opportunity (GOEO). Whether the Nilson Foundation follows a similar funding path, or remains privately funded as its founders have said they intend, is an open question this outlet continues to track. Nothing found so far indicates the Foundation has sought or received any public funding — this is a question being asked in advance, not an allegation.

What's still unconfirmed

Who specifically administers the Nilson Foundation's Facebook page has not been independently confirmed. Whether the organization has since filed Form 1023 with the IRS is also unconfirmed — the IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search reflects determinations, not pending applications, so a filed-but-not-yet-approved application would not necessarily appear there. Neither gap changes the two facts that are independently confirmed: the "501(c)(3)" and "tax-deductible" claims were made, without qualification, on marketing materials — and continue to be made today.
